First attempts at buying gold abroad

I can tell you about smuggling too, though it was long ago. It was probably my first trip abroad — yes, a business trip abroad to buy gold. I went to Turkey with a friend from Sochi, the nearest departure point for us. From Sochi there were “Raketa” hydrofoils, just like the ones on the Soviet-era tourist route to Peterhof. Such a boat took four hours on the Sochi – Trabzon route. You needed nothing, no visa (Turkey still requires no visa today). You buy cheap tickets, board, pass customs and off you go.

My friend and I rested in Sochi for a couple of days. We took about a thousand dollars to Trabzon — probably half the family budget. And we decided that since we were going to Turkey, we’d go on to Istanbul, buy gold there and bring it back… Smuggling was nicknamed “kontrabas” (double bass), but we didn’t even know that slang word; we simply planned to bring back what we bought and hand it in to a shop. So off we went to Trabzon. Summer, good weather… We sunbathed, put on our best clothes and set off. It was probably 1995, or maybe earlier, 1993.

We arrived in Trabzon. We could have flown to Istanbul — a Trabzon – Istanbul ticket cost about fifty dollars. But I thought Turkey was small (I hadn’t studied the map!), and the bus from Trabzon to Istanbul cost only twelve dollars. We decided: we’ll take the bus, Turkey is small, not like Russia, we’ll get there. We bought tickets and got on. But I didn’t expect it — I was amazed myself: the journey to Istanbul took twelve hours (!), our feet swelled… Yes, there were stops — for coffee, for washing feet (Muslims have special foot-washing fountains) — every two hours. By the time we reached Istanbul we were dazed by that twelve-hour ride.

We walked round the little shops, compared prices, thought about what to buy where. Mostly we wanted chains and zodiac pendants. We rented a hotel — not a hotel, really, just a room for the night, not even for a full day, for five dollars. The window looked onto a rubbish dump; in fact it was right in front of us — you look out of the window and see a rusty bin… Anyway, we rented a room for two for the night for five dollars, and even haggled.

On the second day we bought everything and walked around the centre. Istanbul is a beautiful city; I liked it very much. I even remember we went into a restaurant: the heat was terrible and we decided to have a beer. Beer cost a dollar, which seemed very expensive to us, but we liked the service: beautiful mugs and glasses, a dignified waiter — I had never seen anything like it. My friend and I each drank an ice-cold mug of beer — it went down so well! We debated for a long time whether to spend another dollar each, and… splashed out — ordered another round.

I don’t remember exactly how much jewellery we bought (maybe a bit under a thousand dollars’ worth) — a fistful of gold, easy to hide under your clothes. Then back on the bus to Trabzon. There we boarded the hydrofoil again, but couldn’t make it in four hours. We were amazed at the volume of goods being loaded: shuttle traders were boarding with us, each with several stuffed bales as big as a car. They loaded the Raketa so heavily that it couldn’t rise onto its foils because of the overload and crawled along for two or three hours, burning diesel. The way back took about seven hours: first we burned diesel for a long time, then somewhere in the middle of the sea the boat got a little lighter, rose onto its foils, and we reached Sochi by night. We went through customs; the metal detectors didn’t beep (though someone told me they don’t beep anyway). That’s how I brought back my little “kontrabas” — and, I repeat, I didn’t even know the word.

We handed the gold over wholesale to a shop in Krasnodar. I no longer remember the details — probably on commission, simply on our passports. Anyway, the deal went through, but I never did it again. Later we went to an Italian trade fair, our purchases and sales grew over the next few years, a supplier appeared who took all that on himself… And then something happened that cured me of “kontrabas”, somewhere around 1995–1998 — but that’s a separate story.

A story from 1992 (it is dangerous to trust strangers)

You asked about airports and stations — where the fraud techniques came from. I got badly burned several times, so I had to think about it… Here’s a story. A month or two after the army I flew from Krasnodar to Moscow, sold vouchers and a few other things, and bought dollars there. Maybe not much by today’s standards — about a thousand — but it was all the family’s money; it seemed an incredible sum that simply made you afraid.

With that sum I took the bus to Vnukovo airport. Of course it wasn’t like it is now: there were ordinary kiosks in some wagon where things were sold, tickets included. I joined the queue and realised I’d have to wait fifteen or twenty minutes. In front of me stood a little man in a suit, with a walking stick — an educated look, a pleasant appearance. I was the one dressed like a tramp — whatever kept me from freezing, in the cheapest muskrat hat sold in the town of Belorechensk.

There were no services like today; you could only find out whether tickets were available by queuing. I don’t remember whether I wanted to buy a ticket at once or ask about seats for the next day. When the queue moved up, I heard the man in the suit ahead of me asking for tickets to Anapa. I think they said there were none, and he started asking when there would be. We got talking, working out how long it takes from Anapa to Krasnodar if you fly into Anapa, and where I needed to fly… He began telling me how his brother had bought chain-making machines and how they had built up their business, then about searches and how they hid the chains. He told me a fascinating story — just the kind of thing that interested me then.

Talking, we came to a liquor shop behind a metre-high metal mesh. My companion asked whether I knew what wine they had there. I said, what wine (I didn’t drink it at all then, especially not in such bottles)! Or perhaps it went like this: someone came up to the kiosk, asked us what wine it was, and left.

My companion told me a couple more interesting cases — how they shipped the chains out, how they sold them. We had left the airport building and saw a shipping container converted into an office, where they issued passports or stamped visas. A man came out, also cultured-looking, in a suit, showed us a passport, asked: “Do you know what this stamp means?” — and left.

I noticed that both this stranger and my companion wore rare, substantial jewellery. And so did the men walking past: one a chain, one a signet ring, one a bracelet. Back then there was no acid or anything else (I didn’t even have scales), yet you needed to buy gold to resell — there were no instruments or means of testing at all — and I had already been burned several times buying “randol”. You spend all the family money, two hundred dollars, on such “gold” — almost the whole family budget — and then you nearly cry, thinking how you let your parents down… Within a month I had come across fake precious metal several times in different places, got burned, and realised all that “gold” came from the same stash.

I can’t tell you much about randol (I never studied its history), except that it is a copper alloy that looks like 585 gold. Some brass tap, I imagine, that swindlers gave a grand name to skilfully deceive trusting fellow citizens.

I had already told my companion that I too had bought dollars, and then suddenly it slipped out: “Those guys who came up to us — are they with you?” And my companion fell apart… I stood there thinking: how did they figure me out back in the airport — that I might have dollars and gold? How did they know I dealt in gold? I panicked and jumped onto a bus: an Ikarus was pulling out of the airport, turning round. And when, as it turned, the front of the bus faced the park near Vnukovo, I saw the whole gang of three in suits. They stood with their heads guiltily bowed, while the lame man with the stick who had been working on me lectured them, waving his arms — explaining the party line, as they say.

So how did they guess that gold would interest me? Why I was flying to Anapa and Krasnodar? It’s very simple, elementary. My line of business was red-hot because there was hyperinflation and people were frightened; they could not keep any money on hand, it lost value at insane speed. You could wake up in the morning and find it gone. So everyone was interested in gold, everyone was interested in dollars, everyone was interested in how someone made chains, and that story worked as bait for anyone with even a little money in their pocket.

What struck me was how subtle and skilful the lame swindler’s approach was: when you get to know someone you’ve stood in a queue with for half an hour, it seems that you yourself took the initiative. You see him being shy, stumbling, talking to the cashier like any ordinary chance fellow traveller… My conclusion was unambiguous: it makes no sense to buy gold at stations, airports and street corners; the risk is very high.

That’s a story from life, from 1992.

On loving jewellery

As for jewellery — which pieces I liked and which I didn’t… I never really liked any; I had no great love for gold. It just happened that a ready-made business fell into my hands, and there was no reason to change anything: I moved from the voucher market to the gold market. Had there been another option, I would have done something else with the same energy and desire to develop it.

The dangers that lay in wait everywhere

We had no need to hide or disguise ourselves: we looked provincial; it was written on our faces that we had been out of the army for two days. There was only one safety rule, which I proposed myself: pass dangerous places quickly without attracting attention… We considered airports, railway stations and bus stations critical points. Otherwise… You hand in the precious metal at the Bronnitsy plant early — at six in the morning (I’m not joking!) — and then pass the time in a library until lunch. Then the return route: first back to the plant to collect a batch of jewellery, then to the station or airport.

It really was no joke; statistics will confirm a certain percentage of violent deaths among those connected with the Bronnitsy plant who tried to earn money the way we did. Armoured cars and secure communications came to the jewellery industry later; at first everything was makeshift, and even the paperwork wasn’t properly done (I, for example, had only some licence from the local administration). So we tried to pass dangerous places carefully and quickly, and then go home.

How important it is to think fast

Once I badly wanted to fly home for New Year. I decided suddenly, so the documents for the goods weren’t ready yet. I thought: I’ll break the rules and fly. But with a Bronnitsy “parcel” there was no way through airport security, especially without documents. I remember pacing the waiting room, nervous… But they only asked me what was in the package; I said high-alloy steel, costume jewellery — and they let me through without inspection. Sometimes you’re lucky like that.

Later all our managers — Adamas’s, at the time — flew with documents, several people across the country. And these flights were always accompanied by half-hour document checks and long talks with the police: they were constantly testing us — would we pay a bribe or not? In short, a commonplace story for those times.

Priceless experience in spotting fakes

As for how we determined whether gold was real… Sometimes by eye: experience and a trained eye helped; you even began to recognise which factory had made a piece — Yerevan or Kostroma, say — and whether it was from the seventies or modern. There were no reagents at all, not even lapis (as a small reagent for checking whether metal oxidises was called then). There weren’t even scales… That’s how it was!

The history of packaging gold parcels for wholesalers

The packaging of Bronnitsy Jewellery Plant products was, frankly, Soviet: large — fist-sized on average — of thick cardboard, sealed. The height of conservatism. To carry a parcel of jewellery weighing one and a half to two kilos you needed a huge suitcase that wouldn’t even fit in a bus cabin; you had to check it into the luggage hold. And you had to carry a weapon, because Bronnitsy wouldn’t release parcels without one. So that suitcase also held a Saiga rifle. You check in your luggage and off you go…

Of course it was inconvenient to deliver goods in bulky, unsightly boxes and send them to shops. When Adamas entered the market, it gave the Bronnitsy plant fierce competition and showed how things should be done: compact, neat packaging, quality design and printing of tags. Adamas products were of high quality too; I don’t remember any complaints about defects. Among the best!

Bronnitsy’s quality was also excellent, but Adamas approached the matter more progressively — in particular, it packed jewellery in individual pouches: fabric bags with drawstrings, very pretty, pleasant to hold.

Yes, Adamas made a big impression then. A powerful company; they always took the largest exhibition stands and knew how to present themselves and talk to buyers. You could say they were the mentors of the modern Russian jewellery business: they showed how to build and organise it, how to negotiate and trade. When you are a distributor for a large company, you naturally depend on them, and at the same time you are pleasantly amazed by their scale. For about five years we worked closely with Adamas and grew under their wing as a wholesaler. They set our prices and gave us a minimal margin, pennies, but they taught us to work precisely and properly, which was important for us. It ended with them assembling, sealing and shipping a separate parcel for each of our shops; we no longer did it ourselves. We skipped checking, and in five years of cooperation there was never a single mistake in a parcel, nothing missing. I always admired how their warehouse worked. In short, the company was superbly organised; there is much to learn from it!

A chance event led to our first jewellery store

For a long time, probably until 1995, we were afraid to open stores. Or simply didn’t see the need: Krasnodar alone had three or four hundred shops. We had no wish to open a new one and elbow our way past competitors. We liked the franchise scheme, under which we worked with more than one and a half thousand stores.

But then one of our customers offered us a store for nothing — we paid only for the counters. It was in the passage between Crocus City and Tvoy Dom, the first building of Agalarov’s complex. Crocus City had no shoppers at all, since it offered only expensive brands, and people from Tvoy Dom didn’t walk through. In short, a dead spot, but free. Without much optimism we decided to try retail. The store was not far from our office.

We made a sign. We didn’t change the design; we laid our goods out on the same counters. The result surprised everyone. We advertised the store to our whole Moscow database — by then we had a mass of customers from several years of work. We gave minimal discounts, ran a promotion, and the store we had no hopes for brought in a fantastic sum — five or fifteen million. It showed the best result of all our stores nationwide, franchise and wholesale alike. We paid no rent and had few staff. We sold only diamonds and gold then. The success of that store completely changed our plans and our attitude to retail.

The loyalty programme and the Lexus

There was a time when we had no franchise stores. We supplied SUNLIGHT-brand jewellery to other jewellery shops. We set up regular deliveries and settled the assortment. But there were difficulties, even though we introduced control of the final price and gave all goods on commission. Stores that sold the most had automatic replenishment.

We were developing normally, but the retail stores had their own customer bases — regular customers they told about new arrivals and collections. Of course we wanted that information ourselves, as part of our big data.

One day on my way to work I saw a billboard with a barcode and a short phone number. At the office I talked to our managers and learned there were lots of services built on such numbers. Anyone who used the short number automatically left their phone number.

Text messages were very cheap then, and we decided to take advantage. After consulting some leading retail marketers (Sportmaster and other companies), we started developing a loyalty programme. We came up with this: right on the jewellery tag we printed, beautifully, “Lexus”, and decorated the displays of our products. All customers who bought SUNLIGHT products could register via the short number within a year and enter a prize draw. Sales assistants also took part, and we watched the sales of our pieces. There was an extra incentive for shop staff: when selling our piece they entered its code and automatically got their phone bill paid. This way we saw the whole base of sales assistants and our customers.

A year passed, and it was time to draw the promised Lexus. Since the draw was officially registered, we invited representatives of Lexus. They didn’t interfere or set conditions; they asked a hundred dollars for stamping the document with their company seal. We talked it over and decided to give the car to the customer who had bought the most pieces of our brand that year. We found this person on social media and looked at where they lived and what they did. It turned out to be a woman who worked for an insurance company. Our marketing director invited her to us and ceremonially presented the prize.

At that very time casinos were being closed across the country, and apparently an order came from above to check all companies running prize draws. We got an inspection too. In the end, the launch of the loyalty programme cost us two or three Lexuses.

Jewellery education in 1990s Moscow

In 1990s Moscow, the first teacher of everyone involved in precious stones and metals was life experience. The Arbat, the stations, other buying points — gold was bought up regularly across the capital, and the phone numbers of sellers and buyers were known. Someone has metal? Agree on the price — buy it.

Then we took the gold (I no longer went myself; I had partners who did that) to the Bronnitsy Jewellery Plant. Delivered, distributed — by then we were working as a proper firm, a wholesaler of Bronnitsy chains. I wanted something more interesting, less primitive. But specialised jewellery education was hard to get in Moscow in those years.

Once I came across information that a scientific centre (or some similar organisation) was running gem-cutting courses. Vague thoughts of diamonds, of something expensive and important, came to mind at once… I decided to get in, but I think I was late to enrol. I arrived at the Academy (today’s RANEPA) when the course was already under way. I only got as far as the cashier’s office where they took the money. The price for a course of a week or so was super-high — around six hundred dollars, I think. A very decent sum, and dollars were worth a lot then.

So I decided to get the diploma bypassing the cashier, because I really wanted to save money. I was sure I could come to a personal arrangement with the teacher. I entered the lecture hall and was stunned: gigantic dimensions, six-metre doors, ceilings about ten metres high. A grand atmosphere, impressive for a provincial. Nerve-racking, but I thought, I must make myself…

When I went in, I saw about twenty students, and at the lectern the lecturer Nona Dronova, who taught the basics of gemmology. I called her; Nona Dmitrievna smiled and came out to me, beyond the door. I proposed paying her four hundred dollars personally instead of six hundred to the cashier. She was understanding and not offended, but said: “Your offer is good, interesting, but please go to the cashier, and then come back here.”

I completed the gem-cutting course and received a diploma. But that moment stayed with me. I felt like a negligent student who would be left with a failed exam unless he got a pass, ready to chase the teacher around the institute with his last strength, almost into the toilets, pleading and complaining about life (just out of the army, or some other good reason)… You see in their eyes that they pity you, you open your record book, you get your pass — and you are happy! I realised then that you could consult Nona Dmitrievna on any question: she will understand like a friend, advise and support you.

Why you should not smuggle jewellery

What did it mean to bring an imported assortment through customs? At the time it was sheer madness. The market was full of smuggled goods (their makers are long known to all), and bringing goods in officially meant paying twenty per cent VAT and twenty per cent duty upfront. Forty per cent!

At the time eighty per cent of goods on the Russian market were imported — from Turkey, China, Thailand — and all of it came in as contraband. Paying forty per cent upfront was impossible from a business point of view. But only if you looked superficially, without thinking it through.

Smuggling is a secret business; you cannot tell many people about it. You have to do it yourself or with someone close — a wife, a lover. The costs were considerable anyway: you have to bring the goods, pay someone for getting them in, and move money abroad by “crooked” routes. On average it came to about fifteen per cent of the value. A sizeable saving, it would seem.

But there are quite a few “buts”: you cannot plan regular deliveries; you will not delegate it to ten buyers working with several manufacturers at once; you will not entrust it to the purchasing department, because logistics are also impossible with contraband. Moreover, transferring money abroad was risky: the outfits that did it kept closing down. So giving up smuggling was probably the right thing to do. It allowed the company to expand, increase the number of suppliers, fulfil orders quickly, sign annual contracts regularly, receive goods with deferred payment, insure its business in China and thus obtain trade credit. When you are in wholesale, all of this is essential for the company’s growth.

How a business can be cured of the urge to break the law

The continuation of the story about Pavlov’s dog and “kontrabas” — how life sometimes beats a craving out of you.

In the nineties there was lawlessness in the country. I don’t know why everyone criticises the authorities now, but those years were a time of total anarchy in the jewellery and tobacco markets — whatever field you take. Of course, we had seen nothing else; we grew up in the nineties and didn’t understand how it could be different. We had not been abroad; we did what life offered us. The share of Russian goods on the market was very small. Everyone imported contraband; there were companies specialising in it. Turkey applied the assay office hallmark — you could simply bring it from Turkey itself. Probably nobody knew what taxes were. It was Anarchy with a capital A.

At that time special units for combating organised crime were set up, with very broad powers. One day I was summoned to give evidence. The conversation was tough from the start: they took my documents and phone, came to our office and found goods without papers. I spent several days at the directorate. I lost the goods, and the whole story cost me and my family dearly — in nerves and in money.

That is how, like Pavlov’s dog, I was cured of “kontrabas”. Since then I have never dealt in goods of unknown origin. Those agencies used harsh methods, but I learned the lesson. I have no bitter aftertaste from that story. Professionals gave me a lesson, and it was the right thing.
