Create your image

When you represent your organisation or business, your appearance and manner create an impression not only of you but also of the company you represent.

As a rule, your potential partners, customers and friends are very busy people. You have just three minutes to make an impression. There is nothing to be done about it: we live at the pace of advertising clips. Professional image-makers try to fit the presentation of a politician or an event into exactly that time. What can you fit into three minutes? A memorable phrase, an attractive picture, the right colour scheme. The people meeting you have no time to think; they take away one memorable detail. They fill in the rest themselves.

Whatever can stir an emotional response in us is remembered better, seems more significant and has a greater effect — in a word, it makes an impression, even when you have no intention of making one.

It is very important to create the image of an exceptionally successful businessperson. Business reputation implies, above all, success in the economic sense. Your partners and customers want to see in you a reliable and confident leader. Self-confidence rests not on objective measures of your intellect, education or conformity to particular standards, but on your belief that you possess all of these.

The ability to make an impression becomes not only the most important communication skill but also the foundation on which all communication is built. To be heard, you must be noticed. Become visible. Memorable. Significant. Engaging. Clear. And you must not only be all this but look it. Do not burden the other person with explanations — they have no time; instead, let your appearance tell them as much as possible about you, and convey visually what you would like to say in conversation. Evoking the right emotions before the relationship begins is the key to successful interaction.

Clothes and a well-groomed look do not guarantee success, but they show your respect for your audience and for your work.

Recalling the early days of the business in the 1990s, Sergey Gribnyakov came to appreciate the importance of his partner’s white shirt. A white shirt and a straight-A student’s face beat the competition

Nona, have I ever told you how my partner and I started working together? He was a straight-A student, a gold medallist, with a phenomenal memory. Without really digging in, he could remember anything. Back then, privatisation vouchers were being exchanged for shares on the exchanges. “Doka-Pizza”, “Agro-Alliance”, “LogoVAZ” — the whole adventure, a million different shares. We bought up vouchers and exchanged them for shares. The exchanges advertised, everything was official, we were exempt from taxes. A free market, in short. I went to see him at his student hostel and we talked. The next morning a friend of ours woke me up and said he was already at the market getting the hang of it. We sat him down, surrounded him with stacks of share certificates and put him in a white shirt. He exchanged and bought vouchers, and people trusted him — he must have had a face that inspired confidence. One day Cossacks came to the market and started chasing off the currency dealers and gold buyers. But they left us alone, and the work went on. We bought vouchers across the whole Krasnodar region and exchanged them on the exchanges in Krasnodar and Moscow. It was fascinating: swapping paper for vouchers, and vouchers for roubles.
