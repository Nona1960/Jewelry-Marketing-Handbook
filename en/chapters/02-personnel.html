Managing people in a jewellery firm

Classic and modern. This chapter draws on time-tested experience. For what has changed today, see “The team today: what has changed” at the end of the chapter.

Before starting to recruit, it is useful for a company to draw up a list of the personal traits it would like to see in its employees. These are the qualities required of a manager: speed, high qualifications, openness, honesty, teamwork, modesty, responsibility and authority. Having these qualities ensures that managers will not favour only “their own people”, which usually means hiring people like themselves. Remember: hiring the wrong person is one of the most expensive mistakes a manager can make. During recruitment it helps to explain on the company website why candidates should choose your company. For example: “The company is developing extremely successfully, and its success benefits every employee — including a share in its profits and career opportunities.”

Stalin’s principle: “Cadres decide everything.” Sometimes they even decide for you. Therefore:

  • Train your staff.
  • Watch your staff’s condition: an employee who is ill or preoccupied with family problems will do more harm than good. Give them leave, help them, replace them for a while.
  • Organise strict quality control (video recordings, listening to conversations, customer feedback, mystery shoppers and so on).
  • Make sure middle managers do not acquire offices of their own but stay on the sales floor and closely watch how staff work.
  • If services are provided on the customer’s premises, always call the customer afterwards to find out how well they were served.
  • Ruthlessly get rid of those who are rude, inattentive or late, whatever it costs you. A customer who could not get your service because you were short-staffed may still come back; a customer who was badly served will never return.
  • Pay close attention to rumours, gossip and opinions about your products and your competitors’ products.

Be sparing when hiring. If an employee demands a raise without backing the request with a wish to improve the quality of their work — dismiss them! If they try to blackmail you in any form — dismiss them! If they argue improperly and behave unethically without apologising publicly — dismiss them!

Raise the pay of those who work hard, intelligently and care about the business. When you raise pay, remember that you will have to pay it — not once, but every month. Quite often in workplace conflicts staff will try to convince you that it is not only about money, yet almost always it is money that solves their problems.

Sergey Gribnyakov has his own view on assessing employees’ potential:

– The most important thing for staff is the ability to learn. A person who is learning is always ready for setbacks and mistakes, but the worst problem for leaders is early success. Someone who believes they have reached their goal and can now enjoy it loses any chance of learning from their mistakes and the twists of fate. Is the ability to learn important to you when hiring top managers? If you see that a candidate is educated but unable to grow in particular aspects of the profession, how do you react?

– You have to work with what you have. As Lenin said, we’ll make the revolution with the people we’ve got. We search and search and hope it will all work out. We don’t give up over such trifles. We move towards the goal regardless of the terrain.

– Suppose a person has trained, has a good education, but doesn’t develop further…

– Sadly, there are many such examples. These people have no drive for progress. What can you do about it?.. Some have no desire at all to grow, either as professionals or as people. Very few are full of inspiration and eager to master new things, so they are worth their weight in gold. It is better to hire someone eager to learn than someone with a good education — that is obvious. Anyone can get a good education: learn the textbooks, repeat them. People with diplomas can even cause more problems. They have pride; they are sure they spent ten years at university on something important. But the world changes every week, and such a specialist ends up overboard. When you correct them or want to work with them as a team and explain that they are wrong, they experience dissonance — psychological and intellectual difficulty. They expected their education to give them a different social status, and disappointment sets in.

Conservatives are overtaken by people who are more flexible and receptive, inwardly tuned to progress, who offer something new every day: ideas, strategies, methods. It is good if such people have both a specialist education and a drive for change and self-improvement. That is wonderful! Such specialists must be protected and cherished.

– Does it happen that you look at a person and think: oh, they’re putting on airs, they’re nothing special…

– It happens with people after they finish an MBA: they are left with a kind of residue, a feeling that they are above those who didn’t. Perhaps only that. But it passes quickly with time.

– A boss must inspire trust in their staff — some experts call this quality the emotional glue that binds a leader and the team. The compatibility of the head and the staff should be visible, and senior management can be proud of it. Do you agree?

– That’s a problem area for me. These are inner qualities, individual traits: some people have well-developed emotional intelligence, others less so. Mine is probably not the best, so there are shortcomings.

Staff with an artistic education who visit international jewellery fairs are vital to a jewellery firm’s development

Sergey Gribnyakov believes that Italian culture inspires and teaches us to see and value beauty

Today the subject of our conversation is the art of Italy and the role of art in modern life in general.

– You recently visited a jewellery fair in Italy. Why are the creations of Italian jewellers loved all over the world?

– If you grew up surrounded by the beauty and cultural riches of Rome and other Italian cities, you absorb it, and it is reflected in everything you create. A strong artistic school has long been a national hallmark of Italy.

– The SUNLIGHT assortment includes collections by Italian jewellery designers. How do you think their work differs from that of designers from other countries?

– The Italians have a rich cultural heritage that absorbed the decorative traditions of antiquity, the Renaissance, the Baroque and Art Nouveau, and a strong sense of family and continuity, thanks to which the feeling for beauty and the skill of working with precious metals and stones seem to be inherited in jewellery dynasties. Whatever the style of Italian jewellery, it always looks prestigious and festive, because the country’s craftsmen love fine work, intricate decoration, bold forms and large stones.

– And Russian jewellery design?

– It has its own unique history, thanks to the art of Ancient Rus, the Baroque luxury of Carl Fabergé’s creations and the Soviet jewellery school. I believe these traditions should be developed; they have potential, although our designers are now influenced by French, Italian and other jewellers who lead the world because they get more media exposure. So our national traditions have faded somewhat. In Soviet times diamond jewellery had its own recognisable, substantial style. That canon has now been lost. Perhaps it is inevitable: with today’s global market, everyone wears the same things.

– You are inspired by Italian architecture, painting and jewellery. What about cinema?

– Yes, I have a favourite film by Paolo Sorrentino — the decadent drama The Great Beauty. It has two main characters. One is played by Toni Servillo: a journalist, writer and socialite who, on his 65th birthday, suddenly starts thinking about the meaning of life. The other is the Eternal City itself: in this film Rome is not a backdrop or a tourist cliché but a participant and, in a way, a dispassionate judge of events. The frame holds the modern city along with ancient and Christian monuments. The film is very beautiful. In general I like Sorrentino’s work. He never has a bad image; every second of screen time is perfect, alive, harmonious. Every film of his I have seen is talented.

– The masters of world culture have earned their laurels. But what about young people? Some say artists may become professionally redundant because of digital technology, a worsening economy and other reasons. Do you agree?

– I believe the profession will always be in demand. Artists simply get new tools, tasks and fields for their talent as times demand — web design, for example. So everyone needs artists: manufacturers, retailers and app developers. Designing a physical store with a good agency costs 5–10 million roubles in Russia, and in the Netherlands from 200,000 to 1 million euros. Artemy Lebedev, the leader of the Russian web-design market, today charges a minimum of 100,000 dollars per order. Isn’t that demand?

– If a person trained as an artist or architect comes to work for you, by what criteria will you judge whether they are a good specialist?

– The same criteria as teachers at an art school or a specialist university: does the candidate have taste? How do they handle proportion, do they understand creative tasks, can they express any of their ideas?

– And are creativity and originality valued?

– I believe that skill is at the very top, and if a person also generates their own creative ideas, there are only a handful of such people, and of course they are in great demand — in web design perhaps even more than in creating new jewellery designs.

What the head of marketing at a large jewellery company should not be

He must be able to reach agreement with the owners and plan his actions together with them. If he takes on big budgets, he must answer for them. And he must understand that the job is not just analytics where he looks at numbers and draws charts.

He must look more closely at the end customer. If customers enjoyed the brand before he came, then after the marketing director arrives they should enjoy it even more and respect the brand more. That is what NPS (Net Promoter Score) tracks.

You have to understand that many people measure NPS but very few really do anything to make it high. People think a lot about themselves and their vanity, and very little about the end customer.

Russian people perhaps simply cannot sell — maybe that is the gift of the Italians and the French. Because of their upbringing, they love no one but themselves. They don’t respect the people who buy. They think buyers need to be fooled, somehow “conned”. They don’t do it sincerely.

They read various foreign books and still nothing works, because from the start they think not about pleasing the owner but about flattering their own vanity, about being cooler and more important, and so little about the end customer. For them the customer comes last. And the end customer senses it and understands it.

In short, they spend money on themselves rather than on the end customers. When you talk to such a manager, he often says nothing about his ideas or how he will help all the customers. He just wants to please whoever he is talking to.

What are the ten deadly sins of marketing, and how do you feel about them?

Kotler has a book, Ten Deadly Marketing Sins. What sins are in it? There are many nuances. I think it is perfectly sensible to read Kotler, and many other marketing books can be recommended.

But if a person didn’t read Kotler ten years ago, then… Everything else is detail; he set it all out fundamentally. Many companies suffer. Successful companies are all alike, and what goes on inside them is alike. Unsuccessful ones each fail in their own way, so there are probably more than ten different sins. It is impossible to gather them all together, systematise them and explain them now.

But what is the main reason companies suffer? Do they fail to calculate their capabilities during very rapid growth, for example, or with expansion plans — or is it something else fundamental?

Clearly it is because of the owners: a fish rots from the head, and every owner has their quirks. Some are less educated, some are hard-working, some make mistakes and correct them. It is different for everyone — millions of different deviations.

Some work alone, some work as a team; some are talented themselves, others are ordinary but have a good team. Everyone has their own qualities.

I liked a talk by Mikhail Fridman of Alfa-Bank. He said: I have this quality — when I interview someone I am hiring, I am so interested that I don’t even ask questions about their specialism or how well they know it. Most likely they know how to process a payment better than I do. What I want to know is what drives them, what guides them, what their inner values are.

That is my talent, Fridman said; I do it better than others, and that is probably why such large-scale projects worked for me. He gave an example, which I will now tell. He was hiring a director for his bank — a director from another bank who came to the interview. Fridman said: work for a while first — he was wary of appointing him straight away — let’s get to know each other; you can join the supervisory board and we’ll see what you propose.

The man said: no, I can’t work like that, in both places; either right away or not at all. Fridman said: then let’s agree on these terms — for a whole year your salary will be small (I don’t remember exactly how much), but if you stay and we agree you will carry on, your salary will be large.

So 80% of his pay came only after a year of work, and before the year was up he could be dismissed at any moment. And the man decides, takes on those risks — he is so confident in himself that he accepts them. Fridman said: that is how I understand that this is a person who can take responsibility; it says something about his character that he makes such a move — he is confident in himself. In short, that is the kind of entrepreneur he is, that is how he hires managers, and that is probably why he can lead. For me it is different, probably harder, but some people have businesses on a huge scale — talented people.

Secrets of managing people

Do not try to be loved by everyone. It is important to be fair and merciful, but relentlessly harsh with anyone who harms the common cause. Harshness, however, should go no further than dismissing the offender. Never be rude or vindictive. A boss must inspire trust in their staff — some experts call this the emotional glue that binds a leader and the team. Does Sergey Gribnyakov agree with this definition? His strength is that he admits his own problems with communication:

– That’s a problem area for me. These are inner qualities, individual traits: some people have well-developed emotional intelligence, others less so. Mine is probably not the best, so there are shortcomings.

– Let’s discuss “dealing with people”. What do etiquette guides advise? If you want to make a good impression, behave naturally in any situation. Avoid extremes. Don’t be too tense or stiff. Don’t be too serious or put on the air of a very clever, very busy person. Show interest in other people, their affairs and problems. Be restrained and tactful. Express sincere approval. Notice everything you like in another person and tell them so. Pay more compliments. Listen attentively. Watch the other person’s body language. Look for common ground, never for differences. Show only positive signs of attention: praise, gratitude, an appreciative look. Avoid ridicule. To be considered decent and well-mannered, rid yourself of excessive curiosity, a quick temper, touchiness and vanity.

In decision-making, concealing facts is regarded as a crime. There is no explanation more foolish than “I decided it wasn’t important”.

The leader must be the most stubborn optimist in the company. Even if everyone realises the task is impossible, the leader must have the courage to reverse the negative trend and find a way out of the dead end.

Never allow anyone to speak badly of you, your company or your colleagues. Without self-respect there is no successful business. Studies of why people leave their jobs most often find these reasons:

  • no chance of promotion;
  • a poor working environment;
  • poor management.

The reasons why people stay are also known:

  • the chance to gain new knowledge and work with the latest technologies;
  • an excellent working environment;
  • outstanding leadership.

It is very important to reward employees who care about corporate values. If the firm makes a profit, its employees should profit too. Every successful initiative by an employee should be recognised — for example, a name plate on their desk reading “You did everything right”.

An overly harsh management style can lead to mass resignations and lower productivity, because employees have no incentive to serve customers well when they themselves are treated as cheap labour. Very often the problem is not the size of salary or benefits but a lack of attention from management, of basic respect and recognition.

Praise staff immediately, without a second’s delay; but if you want to reprimand them, pause first. Criticise staff only in private. Do not dwell on mistakes endlessly: one mention is quite enough.

Criticise actions, not the person. Explain that you value the employee’s personal qualities but are not satisfied with their work. Say that you hope the situation will not happen again.

Never demand that someone act “as they were told”. Say that you need their cooperation and ask for their help. Admit that you made similar mistakes in the past (this makes your criticism easier to accept) and offer your solution. By admitting your own imperfection, you become a role model in the employee’s eyes.

Thank the employee for helping to solve the problem. End the conversation on a friendly note.

Successful communication is a social game. To master it, you must develop the ability to study and understand people. You need to understand the motives behind their actions and see through the dusty veil with which people often surround their affairs. Understanding people’s hidden motives is a whole science that lets you block endless opportunities for deception and manipulation.

Being too active in society lowers your value. The more you are seen and heard, the more ordinary you seem. Remember: a great deal depends on your absence or presence. A vivid presence attracts power and attention to you; you shine and eclipse others. But after a while the opposite happens: the more you are seen and heard, the faster your influence declines. People get used to you. However hard you try to be extraordinary, people value and respect you less and less. You must know how to leave at the right moment, before you quietly cease to exist for those around you. Learn to tell when to withdraw. Create value through scarcity.

Mastering the game of relationships requires a particular view of the world, a change of perspective. It will take effort. You will have to master a number of basic skills, but once you have, you will be able to apply the rules of the game easily and naturally.

The most important of these skills is controlling your emotions. An emotional reaction to a situation is the biggest obstacle to power. It is a mistake that will cost you far more than any momentary satisfaction from expressing your feelings. Behind emotions you will not see the situation clearly, you will be unable to prepare for it or to control your own reaction to it in any way.

The most destructive emotional reaction of all is anger: it completely robs you of perspective. If you are trying to defeat an adversary who has wronged you, it is far better to lull their vigilance with friendliness than to show your anger.

The ability to manage your feelings is linked to the ability to step back from the present moment and think objectively about the past and the future.

It is very important to learn to conceal your intentions. Display to people an object you want them to think you desire, a goal you appear to be pursuing — your adversaries will believe them real and follow a false trail, making mistake after mistake in their calculations.

Another powerful tool for sending people down a false trail is feigned sincerity. People easily mistake sincerity for honesty. If you show that you believe what you say, your words gain enormous weight. Keep a sense of proportion and try to be convincing and truthful, or your ploy will look like the deception it essentially is. To make feigned sincerity an effective way of hiding your true intentions, assure the other person that you believe in honesty and openness as important social values. Make public statements. Emphasise your position on the matter, now and then voicing some heartfelt thought — one of the meaningless or irrelevant kind.

Never show that you are in a hurry: haste betrays an inability to manage yourself and your time. Always try to appear calm. Learn to choose the right moment and catch the spirit and drift of a conversation. Learn to step aside when the time has come.

If you want to impress with your speech, remember: the more you say, the more ordinary and powerless you will seem. Even when uttering banalities, you will appear original if your words are vague, unfinished and mysterious. Powerful people impress and intimidate by saying less. People always want to know what you are thinking. Your monosyllabic answers and silences will make others nervous and force them onto the defensive. They will start filling the pauses with their own remarks, giving away their secrets and weaknesses. They will leave the meeting feeling robbed, recalling your every word. Such heightened attention to your brief remarks will only add to your power.

The more you talk, the more likely you are to say something foolish.

The team today: what has changed

It used to be considered right to hire salespeople “off the street” for their looks and experience, train them on the job under a senior colleague, and motivate them mainly with a sales commission. It worked, but life has moved on: customers now arrive having already studied the catalogue in the app, and salespeople compete with reviews, videos and AI assistants. Today a team is a group of experts who know more than the customer found online.

ThenNow
The salesperson talks about the productThe consultant helps choose and explains what the website cannot: the stone, the fit, the care
Training once a year at a seminarShort lessons on the phone, quizzes, reviews of real sales, a mentor
Motivation is commission onlyCommission plus customer ratings, repeat sales and professional growth
The company chooses the employeeThe employee also chooses the company: the employer brand matters
Marketing is done in the officeStaff are the face of the brand: their videos and advice work as advertising

Employer brand. Young specialists choose a company by what it will teach them, how it treats people and what they can be proud of. SUNLIGHT’s history, its own production and the Scientific Gemmological Centre are strong hiring arguments and belong in job ads and social media.

Micro-learning. Instead of a thick manual, 5–10-minute lessons with a quiz on the phone: a new stone, a new collection, the rules for labelling synthetic diamonds. This textbook’s glossary and quizzes can be used exactly this way.

The consultant as expert and author. A salesperson who can show a piece briefly and beautifully on camera is an invaluable employee. Videos from the store, answers in chat and “how to choose” guides earn more trust than studio advertising.

What still holds. Taste, a trained eye, trips to fairs and the leader’s personal example matter more than ever. The tools have changed, not the principle: people with good taste must be developed.

Summary and exercises

Key points

  • A jewellery business rests on people with developed taste: an art education and trips to international fairs are an investment, not an expense.
  • An eye for beauty can be trained: Italian culture and the world’s jewellery schools teach people to see and value it.
  • A head of marketing must understand the product and the customer; a “theorist” who does not know the jewellery is dangerous for a company.
  • Managing people is a system: selection, training, motivation, clear rules and the leader’s personal example.
