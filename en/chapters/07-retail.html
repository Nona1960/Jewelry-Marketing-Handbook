For the manager of a jewellery store there are only three areas in which unconditional success must be achieved:

  1. Finding new customers.
  2. Increasing the number of purchases made by one customer.
  3. Increasing the frequency of purchases.

Success in any one of these areas, or all of them together, gives customers a reason to keep using your store, and that in turn leads to the company’s growth.

The US National Retail Federation suggests that the success of a jewellery store depends on a number of factors, including:

  • convenient parking;
  • the demographic make-up of the local population;
  • an attractive shopfront and location;
  • proximity to busy places;
  • sales volumes;
  • safety and reliable security of the building;
  • a broad product line;
  • a catalogue of jewellery that can be ordered.

The main goal in working with customers is to create a positive impression of the store even if they buy nothing on their first visit. A potential buyer will come back to you to buy something unusual. Besides selling jewellery, give potential buyers other reasons to visit the store: meetings with interesting people, contests and other events indirectly related to the product. Do everything to make customers happy at these events. Interesting content and excellent service make a visit memorable. The same happens when you give customers the chance to leave a comment in a special guest book.

It is very important to personalise service for regular customers. Customers might buy books about gemstones. One very successful company sends its customers gifts — collectable stones — and makes sure there are no duplicates in each customer’s collection.

When working, especially with regular customers, it is important to take all these aspects into account. A discount system is also important. The quality of service must be the highest. And it is important to provide individual service in fulfilling orders.

Price, quality, availability, service — all of this matters, but competitors can copy it. What is truly unique is the brand. A brand is a guarantee of quality, proper service and the true value of the product, proven over time and repeated use. Like the marks that confirm the authenticity of a work of art, a brand name should be associated with the solid reputation of the maker or supplier.

Customers sharing positive impressions helps a company succeed. A positive emotional impression is the pleasure people get while shopping. Customer satisfaction is simply a matter of service quality. If service meets customers’ expectations, let alone exceeds them, it helps you keep customers and even build relationships in such a way that competitors cannot lure them away, even with lower prices.

The financial losses from customers defecting to competitors are five or six times — perhaps more — greater than the cost of keeping existing customers. So satisfying regular customers is an important indicator of profitability.

One consulting firm claims that retaining 5% more of a jewellery store’s regular customers can increase profitability by 125%.

Working with regular customers’ orders is important. But satisfaction comes not only from speed and accuracy of fulfilment but also from attention to customers’ expectations. When a casual shopper who wandered in gets exactly what they want, the retailer can turn them into a regular customer and then into a devoted fan of the firm. A group of such fans can attract more and more new customers. It is important that people speak well of your firm.

Despite all the techniques for creating a product name, the most important and effective thing for a company’s success is creating the corporate style, or image, of the jewellery store. For example, the image of a chic store advertising in expensive magazines can be damaged by an ordinary sales assistant who is rude to a customer. Image-building must accompany the product along the whole chain from manufacturer to customer.

There are areas of work that can raise a jewellery store’s image and competitiveness:

  1. Strategic marketing: choosing the store’s image model; choosing new store formats.
  2. The marketing mix: product and assortment strategy; pricing strategy; the price-setting system; discounts and preferential prices.
  3. Purchasing marketing: the system for selecting suppliers.
  4. Purchasing policy: focus on local domestic producers; on importers; on low-priced goods; on buying small batches; on assortment purchasing; on buying well-known brands.
  5. Service strategy: guarded parking; a children’s corner; a café; currency exchange; gift wrapping; jeweller’s services.
  6. Advertising strategy: various types of advertising.
  7. Events.
  8. Store interior.
  9. Staff image.

What is service in a jewellery store? It is not only extra free services; it is the attitude to the customer, a certain business etiquette, the professionalism of the staff. Indicators of high service quality in the jewellery business include:

  • staff training;
  • a quality guarantee on all goods offered;
  • fast, efficient communication with suppliers and customers;
  • quick response to calls;
  • handling customer complaints;
  • respect for customers (thanking them for a purchase, greeting everyone who comes in).

Secrets of success for the head of a jewellery wholesaler

The main priorities for the head of a jewellery wholesale firm are the product, the customers and the sales channels.

Product. Many companies single this out as the main thing, although they owe their success not to the end product at all. It is important to sell not just the item but a lifestyle. Today it is not prices that compete but whether the product suits you. If it suits you, you think about where to find the money. If it doesn’t, you begrudge even a penny and turn away. “Something cheaper, please” has gone out of fashion.

Customers. You need to know as much as possible about your customers to be ready to meet their needs. The head of the business should meet customers as often as possible, not just at a few business meetings. You need to collect data on customer requirements. Many jewellery wholesalers know their market as a whole but very little about their specific customers. Treat each segment of customer relationships as a valuable asset and grow it by finding out what matters to each segment. Customers should tell you what is important to them. Ask them, try to understand them. Look into the buyer’s interests at every stage of the deal. Information about customers should be organised into three groups. Requirements are the properties of jewellery that customers must get. Expectations are product or service standards customers might have expected but did not receive. Wishes are what customers would like to have but do not really expect.

Know the factors that influence customers’ decisions. Distinguish not only each factor but its relative importance. You can ask consumers to describe a situation in which they were very pleased or, on the contrary, utterly dissatisfied.

If you study several groups of consumers, you must identify the few product features most important to each group. Develop questionnaires so that you constantly get fresh data on how consumers rate the firm’s capabilities and performance against competitors.

Sales channels. Benchmarking and comparing your organisation with competitors will help you find your own distinctive approach to sales. Once the research is done, draw up an action plan. The main result of research is that it shows you the direction of necessary changes and improvements in sales. If the results do not lead to changes within the organisation, your efforts are wasted.

When analysing how your jewellery is distributed, answer these questions:

  1. How does each piece reach the market?
  2. Is the transport scheme for finished goods optimal?
  3. What should warehouse stocks be?
  4. Is it better to sell directly or through a network of intermediaries?
  5. What batch size is optimal for sales?
  6. How do you rate the number and qualifications of your sales staff and intermediaries?
  7. How successfully do they sell your jewellery (revenue, costs, number of customers served, etc.)?
  8. Are dealers and other intermediaries provided with all the information they need?

In researching the jewellery market, first determine the type of market for each type of jewellery. The following classification can be used:

  1. A market with no prospects for the given jewellery (e.g. cities with high unemployment for pieces costing 200–500 dollars).
  2. The main market, where most of the jewellery is sold (reputable jewellery stores in city centres).
  3. A supplementary market that absorbs a small share (separate sections in large stores).
  4. A growing market with real potential for increasing sales.
  5. A potential market with opportunities and prospects for sales, but only under certain conditions.
  6. A selective market chosen to test the prospects for a particular type of jewellery.
  7. An inactive market where trade is unstable but which could become active under certain conditions.

Market research is divided into several stages, whose analysis answers the key questions:

  1. what jewellery is bought;
  2. at what price;
  3. who buys;
  4. why they buy;
  5. how the purchase is made;
  6. when they buy;
  7. where they buy.

Competitor analysis involves answering these questions:

  1. What are competitors’ plans for market share, profitability and sales growth?
  2. What market strategy are your competitors currently following?
  3. By what means do they implement it?
  4. What are their weaknesses and strengths?
  5. What can be expected in future from current and potential competitors?

When considering sales promotion methods, answer these questions:

  1. Is a corporate identity used?
  2. Is the trademark attractive enough?
  3. How are different sales promotion methods chosen and evaluated (credit, purchase discounts, bonuses, payment guarantees, preferential deals)?
  4. Are exhibition sales, fairs and consumer conferences organised?
  5. Are consumers and sales agents invited to the firm?
  6. How does sales staff pay depend on jewellery sales?
  7. What is the incentive system for sales intermediaries?

To meet customer requirements fully, keep asking yourself:

  • Have we organised our customer relationships effectively?
  • Do we monitor the quality of our service and evaluate our work?
  • Do we monitor the quality of our interaction with customers?
  • Do our competitors have an advantage we lack? If so, why? Have we missed our opportunities?

There are several typical mistakes jewellery wholesalers make in the market:

  1. Companies ignore the market. They are so focused on the product that they forget about customers. It is important not only to make pieces but to make sure they are exactly what customers need.
  2. Companies that, on the contrary, try to satisfy every customer wish — which cancels out any effort.
  3. Companies suffer from excessive self-regard. While some blindly follow customers with no clear strategic plan, others ignore them altogether.
  4. Companies driven by short-term interests stop sensing market changes. It is important to sense a market downturn in time and move into other segments.

When buying new jewellery collections, the head of a wholesale company should consider:

  • How closely the new line relates to the main one.
  • Whether it will have a positive effect on the company’s core business.
  • Whether it will disappoint regular customers.
  • Whether the new brand will swallow the main line.

The successful jewellery store

How do you make an expensive jewellery store even more expensive without losing customers — indeed, attracting as many as possible? Store owners in every country face this question. French specialists have developed their own concept. Without rejecting traditional methods (advertising, sales), they stress that the store should intrigue the buyer with a new architectural solution — some “special touch”. For example, for the windows and interiors of one French store, designers recommended unusual ultra-modern tables on tall legs as such a touch. This creates a unique style, filling the space around the pieces.

The jewellery store interior

The interior is mostly chosen and planned individually. Selecting and placing equipment and furniture of the right size and type in a limited space is a particularly acute issue. In designing a store, the sales area must be considered so as not to overload it with equipment. A person will not feel comfortable in a small store seeing their reflection on every side; they feel lost in such surroundings.

When designing a jewellery store with a large sales area, consider zoning and how quickly customers take in the goods. Create gentle turns and small obstacles along the customer’s path that make them linger, stop and look at the displays. Gentle turns break the monotony of long uniform rows, shift the visitor’s attention from one group of goods to another and keep them from getting bored.

One requirement: customers must move freely around the sales floor and be able to see clearly all the goods on display. It is very fashionable to place the sales counter in the centre of the store.

Some stores use the central space of the sales floor as the main display stand, around which customers can easily “walk in circles”, studying the collections from every side.

From the point of view of information-led promotion, a modern jewellery store should be a kind of exhibition, a spectacle, a cultural and educational venue where customers can not only buy a product and learn about it but also come away with a positive impression. To this end, specialist stores are advised to set aside a special place in the hall or windows for themed displays. They should change regularly, say once a month. Themes vary with the products on offer, for example:

  1. Seasonal themes.
  2. Holiday themes.
  3. Presentation: a story about the maker and production technology.
  4. Geography: pieces from different countries and regions.
  5. New products, new designs, designer pieces.
  6. Quality guarantee: how to spot a fake and what makers do to protect their brand.
  7. Pieces for specific occasions (weddings, anniversaries).
  8. Products that have won awards and diplomas at exhibitions or competitions.

If space allows, you can give additional, attractively designed information — for example about birthstones for each month or zodiac sign. You can quote legends and traditions associated with particular stones.

Even if no purchase is made, what matters is that the visitor, having gained interesting new knowledge and emotional impressions, buys the piece they liked next time.

It is very important for jewellery stores to have promotional materials to hand out (brochures, leaflets, catalogues) or to sell specialist magazines and books about jewellery.

When designing the interior, remember your target audience, their tastes and interests. The visitor’s first impression of the sales floor matters a great deal. To make it pleasant — or stunning — pay attention to the floor: it can be inlaid ornamental parquet or a beautiful carpet.

Today the mirror is becoming an indispensable attribute of a jewellery store, both as a functional object and as part of the interior. Mirrors can adjust the perceived size of a room. An oval mirror elongated vertically increases the height of a room, while the same mirror hung horizontally widens it.

Jewellery stores need mirrors on counters for trying on chains and earrings — but never inside the counters. Jewellery is usually laid out quite densely. Gold, diamonds, the glass top of the counter — if all that sparkle is added to a mirror reflecting reflections, the result is chaos.

Designers consider mirrors a strong interior device, but they should be used in moderation. Quite often a building’s architecture does not allow some details, such as arches or columns, to be changed or removed. They can then be covered with mirrors, visually getting rid of them: the arches and columns simply “disappear” from view and become transparent.

Large tilted mirrors in antique-style frames look interesting in an interior. Such a mirror stands as if by chance and may not match the fittings at all, yet looks very striking. Many jewellery stores occupy long, narrow, tunnel-like rooms; a mirror will help there too.

For mirrors fixed to walls, glass 8–10 mm thick is recommended so that it can withstand a blow without shattering. For stores with low ceilings, mirrored ceilings are ideal: they visually increase the height of the room.

Walls look good decorated with reproductions or interesting photographs. Large black-and-white photographs have been fashionable recently.

The quality of shop fittings is very important. They must match the quality and price level of the goods, because the customer does not separate the perception of the display from the perception of the product — for them it is a single visual image.

The most common jewellery stands are matt materials in a baked-milk shade (with or without a leather-like emboss) framed in wood or wood-effect, or covered in black or burgundy velvet. Despite velvet’s excellent ability to set off the product, it is seen as slightly old-fashioned and “weighs down” the interior. The main thing in choosing stands is a single style for the whole store and consistency with the overall interior design.

Overly bright colours in the hall make customers anxious and excited, and too many of them irritate. But bright colours as separate accents arouse interest.

Monotonous, grey or overly pale décor is usually associated with everyday worries and does not help sell jewellery. The most effective is considered a combination of dark and light or bright tones.

Bear in mind that an illuminated counter acts as a bright spot that draws the main attention, so it must not be overpowered by a more striking wall colour or other interior detail.

American specialists believe that interior colour strongly affects the psyche of customers and staff. Pink, for example, suppresses vital energy, while red, orange or yellow raise it. But too much orange can cause apathy. Green relaxes and calms; it suits sales floors with a calm design. And blue favours an atmosphere of business-like communication between customer and seller.

In recent years many well-known jewellery companies have designed their stores in a minimalist style. Minimalism is not just an interior style but rather a way of life. There is something of Zen Buddhism in it: the same freedom from endless bustle that leaves no time for deep thought. Arranging our living space by the laws of minimalism, we get rid of everything superfluous that keeps us from living freely. Specialists say this style is preferred above all by creative people, who find it easier to think in a minimalist interior. Besides, anything left unsaid stirs fantasy and develops imagination.

Where does minimalism begin? With limitation. It is the art of subtraction. A minimum of furniture, decoration and accessories, but plenty of light space in which the eye glides easily from one object to another. It would seem nothing could be simpler: throw out everything superfluous, leaving only what is needed for work, and you have minimalism. But alas, the simplicity of minimalism is only apparent. Behind it lies an endless search for the perfect form, defined by strict geometry of line and flawless proportion.

Another important law of minimalism is functionality. A refined, precise form is good not in itself but above all because it determines comfort — the quality of life. Natural, environmentally friendly materials — wood, stone, glass, leather, metal — processed with the latest technologies play a large role. The minimalist palette is restrained and almost monochrome: its favourite colours are white, black and all shades of grey and brown. Bright colours are quite acceptable, but only a few. A minimalist interior resembles graphic art.

Light plays a special role in minimalism — soft, diffused, flowing freely through the space. Large windows are a great asset.

Minimalism may seem too austere, cold and even soulless for jewellery retail. On the contrary, such a style only emphasises the sensuality and warmth of jewellery, especially exclusive pieces. For the few display cases, preference is given to wood in rich dark-brown, chocolate shades typical of rare exotic woods — wenge, ebony or mahogany.

Many mistakenly believe a minimalist interior requires a large space. Not at all. The simple, laconic silhouettes and restrained colour scheme of this style suit a small jewellery store perfectly well.

As for lighting jewellery, “local” lighting matters far more than the room’s overhead light. Soft, even subdued overhead lighting creates an atmosphere of calm and leisure that invites contemplation, while more brightly lit display cases draw customers’ attention. In the USA dimmed light was used in jewellery stores for a long time, but practice in recent years has shown that a brightly lit space is more attractive to customers.

When deciding on store lighting, remember that it should:

  • enhance the brilliance of diamonds and metal and deepen the colour of gemstones, making jewellery shimmer;
  • emphasise the naturalness of materials used in the interior (wood or marble, for example);
  • project the sparkle of precious metals and stones attractively onto mirrored panels.

Based on these principles, many designers prefer numerous halogen bulbs built into the ceiling. Combined with miniature lamps around the hall, they make the interior bright and festive.

In lighting a store, take into account these features of stones and metals:

  • platinum needs soft light;
  • silver loves cool tones;
  • yellow gold looks best in warm light;
  • sapphire, tourmaline and black diamond come alive in daylight;
  • ruby, emerald and diamond are beautiful under electric light;
  • blue sapphire dulls under electric light.

Remember: with the right lighting, stones should shine.

To keep the air fresh, the sales floor should be ventilated. Plants also help create an atmosphere of freshness. But avoid strongly scented plants, flowers and air fresheners, as customers may have allergies.

American specialists suggest limiting outside noise on the sales floor — traffic from the street, phones ringing in the office — which prevents people from concentrating fully on their purchase.

To solve this, lay carpets and soundproof the walls and ceilings of the sales floor.

Music should be calm and unobtrusive. Loud, overly “pressing” or “heavy” music will make customers leave rather than look at the displays. So-called background music — slow, without lyrics — relaxes customers and puts them in a contemplative mood and, according to statistics, raises sales by 30 per cent. People do not want to leave; they feel comfortable, rest from daily stress and “buy an illusion” of comfort, unhurriedness and the attainability of the unattainable. In such an atmosphere visitors buy more often. The sound quality must be excellent and the source of sound “diffused”.

What kind of music?

Classics in modern arrangements (Mozart, Chopin and others), light jazz, blues; sounds of nature (a waterfall, the forest, etc.). If you want to highlight a collection from a particular country, you can play that country’s national melodies. Then the music creates an extra atmosphere that awakens interest in the goods on offer.

Highly professional sales staff give status to a salon with expensive jewellery. It is desirable for sales assistants — most of them women — to wear the store’s uniform, which should be strict and business-like but with something unusual about it. Every employee should wear on the breast pocket a plastic badge with a photo, job title and full name. Job titles are best written as: section manager, sales consultant, expert gemmologist. This inspires respect and confidence that the customer is dealing not with an ordinary seller but with a professional. The lettering on the badge must be clear. The store’s trademark is a good addition.

The area for trying on expensive jewellery (the VIP area) must be cosy and intimate. Ideally it is a small separate room. If there is none, create a secluded corner in the main hall. Fill the space with the scent of fresh flowers, put out sweets and gentle music, add good lighting. The sales assistant must be able to talk professionally about the uniqueness of the chosen piece. Ask questions, listen attentively, stay calm, smile, and bring pieces to try on that match the customer’s requirements.

For a long time sweets were the main treat in jewellery stores. Now it has become fashionable to put fresh fruit on the table instead — tangerines, apples, bananas, grapes, plums, oranges — or dried fruit: raisins, dates, prunes, dried apricots.

There are no trifles in jewellery retail; even the smallest details matter enormously. Clean fittings, especially glass, and neat price tags and stands play no smaller a role in customers’ perception than a well-chosen assortment.

Pay attention to the area around the store so that it does not become an obstacle for potential customers.

Clear puddles, snowdrifts and ice promptly. Staff must not smoke on the steps.

The area must be well lit — this gives visitors a sense of safety and comfort.

Organising the jewellery store counter

A customer always picks out, “snatches” one item from the collection first; the other pieces become background for a while. Use this rule if you want to focus the customer’s attention on a particular product to promote it.

A particular piece can be highlighted with lighting. There are several rules for attracting attention to jewellery:

  1. People notice some colours faster than others — bright red, yellow, pink. Customers will see pieces with stones of these colours sooner than pieces with dull or cool-toned stones; place pieces with less vivid stones in the best spots on the counter, otherwise they will be “lost”.
  2. People always tend to notice the new and unusual first, so a non-standard piece of original shape will be noticed sooner. Original pieces should be placed roughly midway between the best and the least favourable spots so that they still catch the eye.
  3. People periodically need to switch their attention, so do not line pieces up in a long, strict row without visual accents: a customer who was not planning to buy that type of piece will simply walk past looking for something more striking. Each group of pieces should have its own “highlight” — something to catch the eye.
  4. People take in information and objects more easily when they are grouped — “sorted onto shelves” — and the grouping must be clear and logical. So goods should be placed in groups on the display and counter, not at random. Ideally they should be grouped on several criteria at once: by brand, by type of product, by size, by price.

Every seller knows that goods at eye level sell much better. So specially promoted goods should be placed exactly at eye level, where people do not have to look for them and will see them anyway. Eye level is a zone ±20 cm from the eye level of an adult of average height, within about 30° of the point of focus — usually the second and third shelves from the top of a standard five-shelf unit. Some authors believe the best-selling and best-known pieces should be at eye level. One cannot always agree: the most popular models are already well known and customers will find them easily even if slightly off eye level. Their sales will fall less than the promoted product’s sales will rise at eye level.

Since jewellery stores use vertical shelving less often and mostly use counters, does eye level matter here? Yes, it does. A counter also forms a visual field, with zones far from the customer (at the sides and on the seller’s side) and zones close to the customer. Eye level here lies between the customer and the central part of the counter — about two-thirds of the width of the display from the customer’s side.

The exact eye level depends on the counter’s tilt (if any), width and height. In this sense very wide counters are unfortunate, as the eye-level zone does not cover their whole width. Watch customers in your store: where are their eyes most often directed when they stand at the counter? Place promoted goods along that line.

It has been observed that objects in the lower part of a person’s visual field are examined less than those in the upper part. So the lower shelves and counters, being the least viewed, should hold rarely bought pieces and large, bright jewellery.

Display cases have “dead zones” of perception — above all the corners farthest from the customer, where the eye rarely falls. The worst is the lower left corner. Human perception is such that the gaze concentrates most and lingers longest in the central part of the visual field — there we see best and look longest. Ideally, avoid placing goods in dead zones (if possible) and avoid displaying jewellery on multi-shelf units. Customers should have no difficulty looking at goods, and for jewellery this is especially important. Practice shows that wedding rings and machine-made chains and bracelets should always be displayed separately and never mixed with anything else on the same counter: they are the most popular personal jewellery and in high demand. As for rings: small and medium sizes are best displayed nearer the customer, larger ones further away. The same applies to expensive exclusive pieces. They should be placed separately from all other jewellery — preferably, if possible, in a separate room. No special information is needed for them. The main rule for displaying exclusive goods is to minimise the number of pieces on show. This does not mean a meagre assortment: the pieces on display should simply signal the idea of the collections available. Better that the customer asks the seller for explanations, and the seller convinces them to buy an exclusive piece.

If the assortment has something special (rings of fashionable design or sets with unusual stones, say), there should be special information about it designed to catch the eye of everyone who enters the store — better still, everyone who walks past.

Jewellery is traditionally grouped by several criteria:

  • by metal: silver, gold, platinum, palladium (gold pieces are further divided into red, yellow and white gold);
  • by presence and type of stones: pieces without stones, with cubic zirconia, with coloured stones, with diamonds;
  • by type of piece: earrings, rings, bracelets, chains, necklaces, pendants, piercing jewellery, etc.

Each group should have its own name and differ visually from other groups (this does not apply to sets).

Primary information, such as “Gold jewellery”, is best placed above the seller’s head so that customers can immediately spot what they need anywhere in the store. Secondary information should complement it — for example, on counters with diamond jewellery, “Certified pieces” and “Platinum pieces” are highlighted separately.

All personal jewellery should be arranged in order of increasing price. Then customers will look through the whole assortment that matches their wishes and means.

To make the display interesting and successful, add a few bright details. Flowers, shells and lace in the window will attract customers and make them look at the whole assortment.

Create compositions in individual display cases — no more than three in the whole store, otherwise they will distract from the displays. Compositions should follow a theme that reflects your store’s style, and they should be refreshed and changed. A sense of novelty can be created simply by rearranging pieces within a group.

If some pieces sell poorly or customers fail to notice them, place them in the centre of a case with popular pieces and they will be noticed at once. This technique is called “borrowed popularity”.

Do not overload pieces with tags. Tags should be neat and easy to read. Next to the pieces place a price tag showing only the figures.

New products should carry a special label.

The following promotional materials can be used:

  • leaflets in holders on days of in-store promotions and to spread information about new products;
  • mini-brochures with product information (placed next to the product);
  • posters — for back walls and outside windows;
  • light boxes — illuminated structures placed above a section;
  • banners — horizontal or vertical, above a department or next to the advertised product.

Promotional materials must not draw too much attention to themselves.

Jewellery is traditionally divided into three price groups — low, medium and high. The rule is that low-priced goods are placed at the beginning of the row (following the main flow of customers), then medium and high. A customer “with money” who has come for an expensive item will in any case reach “their” counter or display, whereas high prices at the entrance may put off a customer of limited means. The price gradation should be gradual: pieces differing several times in price should not be placed side by side — the cheaper one will sell better, but the expensive one will lose its appeal.

The jewellery store window

The window is the face of the store. It must attract the attention of potential customers and be memorable. Designing a window well is an art not given to everyone.

The main functions of a window:

  • informing customers about the goods;
  • showing new jewellery;
  • advertising the brand;
  • emotional and aesthetic impact on the customer;
  • offering additional services.

Rotating stands make a striking window — best of all solar-powered ones that need neither mains nor batteries. Rotating pieces are more likely to draw visitors to the window and let them see a piece from every side.

Today glass display cases for jewellery stores are recommended to be as wide and tall as possible, made of strong laminated glass.

Lighting the window plays a special role. In general, correct lighting is fifty per cent of success; dark windows attract no attention. It is also important to decide in advance which goods to place in the window and how. First, place the goods that best embody the corporate style. Second, the models in the window must be properly lit. And the window “interior” should set off the models: if they are dark, the wall behind them should be light, and vice versa; otherwise they may get “lost”.

One trivial but very important point: the models in the window must be on sale. If someone likes a piece in the window but cannot buy it, they will become wary of all windows. And quite obviously, the window must be clean.

Your jewellery store’s address

Location is very important in the jewellery business. The address chosen by Van Cleef & Arpels, for example, proved remarkably fortunate: 22 Place Vendôme. In 1906 the house was the first to understand the significance of this place, now called the “jewellers’ square”. All around are wealthy districts, and the Ritz hotel is on the square itself. Soon the firm opened branches in fashionable resorts — Deauville, Vichy, Nice.

In 1910 the Fabergé shop was in the firm’s own building in an aristocratic district of St Petersburg, on Bolshaya Morskaya Street. The shop windows were always half-curtained: Carl Fabergé did not think it necessary to display things in the windows. Works of art and valuables were often shown to visitors by the owner himself, and when he saw a connoisseur in a visitor he devoted a good deal of time to them, even knowing they were not a buyer — showing his works, telling their history and sharing his thoughts about what he was working on.

The Harry Winston salon, which sells jewellery with unique diamonds, is on Fifth Avenue in New York. The sign over the imposing main entrance proclaims “Rare Jewels of the World”. In the small vestibule a polite, strict young woman in a black dress, black stockings and black shoes looks clients over carefully, while a hidden camera films. A client who passes the check, or a famous and truly wealthy one, is led through a screened door into the salon: a tall, spacious room with an exquisite chandelier, a black carpet with white criss-cross lines, seven austere display cases of necklaces and earrings on the walls and only a few gemstones besides. There is little furniture — just five antique ebony tables, at each of which sits a middle-aged man in a black suit, white shirt and sober tie, with a young woman in black beside him, ready to bring from a hidden safe any jewel the client might wish to see. The black background is no accident: nothing must distract the client from the brilliant sparkle of the stones. Buying a magnificent gem in the salon is no simple matter. For most visitors, the owner Richard Winston explained, it is a remarkable event, and the entrance was deliberately made especially imposing so that, once inside, clients are in no hurry to leave.

Summary and exercises

Key points

  • Wholesale and retail need different skills: the wholesaler needs reliability, terms and logistics; the retailer needs the customer and atmosphere.
  • A successful store is a match of address, interior, window, assortment and staff.
  • The interior and the counter should lead the customer to the jewellery: lighting, route and comfortable try-on.
  • A window is free advertising 24 hours a day; it is changed regularly and thought out as a story.
