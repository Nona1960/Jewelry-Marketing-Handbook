Merchandising

More and more jewellery stores are applying strategic merchandising methods in practice. The English word comes from “merchandise”, meaning goods or trading.

Merchandising is marketing inside the store; its aim is to carry out a maximum programme and a minimum programme.

The maximum programme involves two main tasks:

  • building and strengthening the store’s image;
  • promoting jewellery brands and products.

The minimum programme focuses on the customer, in order to:

  • increase the time they spend in the store;
  • encourage a purchase;
  • encourage repeat purchases;
  • encourage impulse purchases;
  • motivate further cooperation with the store.

The key to a successful sales force is selecting truly useful salespeople. The businessman McMurry wrote that he was convinced a person with the gift of a great salesperson is a born “wooer”, someone with a compelling need to win and to bind others to themselves. He also named five additional traits of a top salesperson: great energy, complete self-confidence, a constant hunger for money, well-honed professional technique, and treating every objection, resistance or obstacle as a personal challenge. In addition, a truly useful salesperson must have at least two basic qualities: (1) empathy — the ability to feel what the customer feels; and (2) ego drive — a powerful personal need to make the sale.

A salesperson must know how to meet and greet a customer so as to lay a good foundation for the relationship. Appearance, first words and subsequent comments all play a part. The salesperson should be dressed roughly as the customer is, and be polite and attentive. They should not stare at or scrutinise the customer.

It is very important to help customers solve their problems. Bear in mind that:

  1. customers may have hidden needs of commercial interest to the firm;
  2. customers value sensible suggestions;
  3. customers will be loyal to a salesperson who takes their long-term interests to heart.

It is very important to attract customers with attractive offers. This means not only a flexible discount system but also a rich assortment and a high level of service.

Training programmes for sales staff

Training programmes for sales staff pursue several aims:

  1. Introducing the employee to the firm so that they identify with it: its history, goals, organisation and management system, its leaders, financial structure, main products and sales figures.
  2. Introducing the firm’s products: future salespeople learn how the goods are made.
  3. Understanding customers and competitors: different types of buyers, their needs, motivations and habits.
  4. Training in effective sales presentations and the main commercial arguments for each product. Some firms give future salespeople scripts for conversations with customers.

It is recommended to hold a staff meeting every three months to remind employees what deserves special attention — making customers feel important and delighted with the service.

One of the most valuable qualities of a good manager is the ability to find a particular talent in every employee and to surround oneself with capable, necessary people.

A company should have its own culture, unique to it. That is something competitors can never copy.

To strengthen team spirit, encourage employees to:

  • See their duties through the company’s common goals. When management and staff understand the company’s main tasks, they have more incentive to act as one team.
  • Know and appreciate the company’s long-, medium- and short-term goals. This helps people feel their importance in the company and link duties to goals — a good incentive to work.
  • Share responsibilities. Teamwork means not only dividing duties but also discussing the work plan and overall results together, so that everyone feels supported by colleagues.
  • Be rewarded for results. When people are praised for a job well done, they want to achieve even more.
  • Learn to make important decisions. Managers should hold regular meetings, testing the team’s ability to identify key problems, plan solutions and act independently.
  • Find a common language. Words do not matter here; what matters is the feeling of unity.
  • Know the company’s traditions. Not that everyone should wear the same uniform, but everyone should know the company’s traditions, history and style of work.

Working with jewellery store customers

To be useful to customers, you must first know who they are — know your target audience. It is just as important to understand what of value you can offer a particular customer and how you can attract them.

There should be a special plan for maintaining the assortment. It is important to sell not only matching sets but a wide range of complementary pieces from one collection from which customers can form their own set. Let your customers create and play. Putting together a set of jewellery is an exciting process for a buyer, especially for a woman. Some prefer a ring and earrings, some earrings and a pendant, some a pendant, ring and bracelet. The chance to buy matching items is one of the most powerful drivers of impulse purchases. A woman who likes a pair of earrings will happily buy a matching ring or bracelet, or even several pieces. A ready-made set often feels like one expensive purchase, whereas separate items feel like two or three inexpensive ones — and the customer does not stop to think that a set assembled individually actually costs more than a ready-made one. Besides, a ready-made set may include a component the customer does not want. Free choice noticeably raises overall sales, and the game-like nature of the purchase increases customers’ enthusiasm and joy.

An experienced seller should know that when a woman enters a jewellery store she wants, above all, to satisfy her curiosity about new fashions, gain new impressions, enjoy pleasant emotions and feel her status and importance. A visit to a jewellery store is also a modern way for a woman to relieve stress by giving a present “to her beloved self”.

It has been observed that during busy pre-holiday trading, the main buyers of expensive pieces with diamonds and gemstones are men. As a rule their knowledge of jewellery and gemmology is limited, so they rely entirely on the seller’s professionalism.

If a man doesn’t know exactly what he needs or is lost among the variety, find out who the piece is for, for what purpose and occasion, the recipient’s age and tastes. Tactfully find out how much the customer is ready to spend. Depending on the answers, suggest a suitable piece. Draw attention to its characteristics and merits and to the maker’s reputation. Always show the product tag and explain the information on it. Support the customer’s choice and emphasise his excellent taste.

The presentation of a product should show the benefits it brings the buyer. It helps to draw the customer into conversation so that he tells you about his needs and his relationship with the person the gift is for; then, having formulated those needs, show exactly how a particular piece will meet them. This requires good listening and quick problem-solving. You must attract the customer’s attention to the product, hold his interest, arouse the desire to buy and secure the purchase.

At the start of such a conversation the customer almost always raises objections, for psychological or logical reasons. Faced with objections, the seller politely and kindly asks the customer to explain them, phrasing questions so that the customer ends up answering his own objections.

Try to show potential buyers exactly the pieces they need. Advise them. Use your experience of past sales. Try to interest the customer and find an approach to everyone.

At the end of the deal your customer should feel important. Make them feel they are the most exceptional person you have ever met — the most talented in the world, who knows better than anyone how to spend their money.

How customers perceive a new piece of jewellery

Pieces from new collections — “new pieces” — should be treated as a separate category.

A new piece is one that some customers perceive as completely new.

The perception of a new piece goes through stages:

  1. Awareness. The consumer learns about a new jewellery design or new stones from fashion magazines, films and so on, but does not yet have enough information.
  2. Interest. The consumer is motivated to look for information about new fashion trends in jewellery.
  3. Evaluation. The consumer decides whether it makes sense to buy an unusual piece.
  4. Trial. The consumer buys one piece of the new design (a ring, say) to form their own view and see what impression it makes on others.
  5. Adoption. The consumer decides to buy the whole set and wear it regularly.

Research has shown that women in Russia from the late 2010s to the 2020s differed noticeably in their readiness to try jewellery of unusual design. Every region and every age and social group has its pioneers and followers. In this respect women can be roughly divided into several categories:

  1. Innovators, who adopt a novelty at once. Most often women aged 26–39 with higher or incomplete higher education, who dreamed as children of becoming actresses or artists, do not consider themselves beauties but love everything extravagant. By horoscope, most often Sagittarius, Pisces, Aries.
  2. Early adopters. Opinion leaders in their circle who take up new ideas fairly early, if cautiously. According to surveys, women aged 23–48, well settled in life and content, who usually consider themselves great beauties and highly qualified professionals. By horoscope, often Taurus, Capricorn, Libra.
  3. Early majority. Prudent women aged 27–50, often with grown-up daughters or close relatives, who read fashion magazines and travel and are rarely leaders at work. By horoscope: Virgo, Gemini.
  4. Late majority. Sceptical women who adopt a new piece only when almost half the town is wearing it. Aged 25–60. Often insecure; when buying jewellery, the main thing for them is to show that they are no poorer or worse than others. All signs of the zodiac.
  5. Laggards. Women too attached to tradition. They are suspicious of change, mix only with other traditionalists and adopt a novelty only once it has itself become a tradition.

The speed with which a piece is adopted is influenced by:

  1. Relative advantage — the apparent superiority of the piece over similar ones (brighter stones, unusual shape, larger size, metal colour).
  2. Compatibility with accepted consumer values and experience. For example, a piece of jewellery must emphasise the owner’s prestige and good taste.
  3. Divisibility — the chance to try the piece in small quantities (buy only the ring from a set, or a piece with commercial-quality stones).
  4. Communicability — how visible and easy to describe to others it is.

Other characteristics affecting adoption include the initial price and social approval.

Every unusual product has its own promotion secrets. The mere presence of pieces in a particular metal in the window, for example, underlines a store’s prestige and automatically places it among the “fashionable” and “advanced”. Foreign jewellery store owners, for whom platinum pieces in the assortment matter a great deal, say that what platinum can do, no other precious metal can. Platinum jewellery raises a store’s status to that of a leader. The distinctiveness, expressiveness and at the same time restraint of a platinum piece are transferred to its owner. An advertising campaign for platinum can draw consumers’ attention not only to a particular store but to its whole assortment. Platinum is a kind of “alternative approach” to promoting other goods: the precious metal acts as a “locomotive”, a driving force. Western marketers stress the importance of prestigious goods appearing in the windows. Among other things, this trains professional staff, since a status product is handled with more care: it is more interesting to work with and demands new knowledge, a new approach to service and a new staff image — all of which strengthens the business. If the store is holding a sale, this must be shown in the window — how else will customers learn of it? It could be a bright sign above the models on sale or markers on mannequins (bright tags or plaques, for example).

How to advise jewellery buyers

Every jewellery store salesperson must understand the buyer’s motivation. To do so, put yourself in your customer’s place and enter into their wishes and preferences.

Jewellery answers a deep human desire, born in ancient times, to adorn oneself. Collecting, using and loving jewels have been driven by emotions ranging from blind superstition to the need for financial security and social status.

Eight things that motivate people at a subconscious level were identified by a marketing agency called Beyond Reason:

  • Certainty — we dislike uncertainty and prefer stability.
  • Belonging — we want to belong to a community or group.
  • Recognition — we like to be valued.
  • Individuality — we enjoy personalisation and like to think we have free will.
  • Power — we want to master something or have influence over it.
  • Self-development — we want to expand our knowledge and become better.
  • Sex — we all have this very basic human desire; but it can also mean closeness in a more platonic sense: family, friends.
  • Physiology — we have basic bodily needs that must be taken care of.

If a jewellery salesperson appeals to one or more of these motivations, they will understand the customer and delight them with the right choice of jewellery.

Now let’s put ourselves in the place of your potential buyer. What questions do they ask themselves?

Today it is not easy to spend hard-earned money on jewellery in a way that brings pleasure and satisfaction. Why?

  1. You have to choose a piece you like.
  2. The choice must respect the rules of good taste.
  3. The piece should emphasise the best of your appearance and hide its flaws.
  4. The piece must be of good quality.
  5. The piece must be bought at a fair price.

So how do you choose? What criteria should guide you?

First of all, decide how much you are ready to spend. This will save you from confusion and temptation later, and therefore from disappointment. I assure you that even if the sum is modest, your choice will still be wide enough. The most important criterion is that you like the piece.

Set aside all other motives. Pay no attention to the name of the stone or its momentary popularity. When choosing, listen to your feelings.

The piece you buy may become your companion for many years. For that, it helps to know as much as possible about the properties and features of stones. It is always pleasant to tell a legend about a gemstone in company, or simply to advise how to choose a stone by its characteristics. Walk round the shops and see what styles of pieces and which gemstones are on offer.

Try on pieces with different settings, especially rings. I have met many people who did not like a ring in the window but changed their minds after trying it on.

It is also good to know that wearing jewellery follows rules of good taste, on the basis of which the author offers her recommendations.

Recommendations for choosing jewellery

Young girls suit jewellery made of inexpensive materials but bold and striking in composition. Older people do better with more modest pieces in more valuable materials. Young girls do not wear diamonds, except perhaps a simple ring with a small diamond on the ring finger of the left hand or delicate earrings. Suitable jewellery for a girl: a few strands of pearls, silver earrings or a ring with coral or turquoise, a small gold watch on a fine bracelet.

Married women may wear all gemstones. A cameo ring does not suit young women but does suit a lady of elegant age.

A petite, delicate woman with long hair can wear romantic jewellery — bows, sprigs, posies…

A full-figured woman should choose classic-style jewellery: nothing loud or bright that draws attention to her figure.

Jewellery should be chosen with the season in mind. Pieces with diamond-like stones or sparkling coloured stones and with imitation pearls are meant for evening dress and mainly for winter.

Sports watches are not worn with evening dress. At the same time, evening is the only occasion for a watch set with diamonds. A diamond necklace may be worn only to a ball and only with a low-cut dress.

Silver pieces are not considered dressy unless they are exclusive. Silver and gold can be combined, but only in a well-thought-out way. A silver necklace should not be worn with a gold bracelet.

A sporty style means jackets and sweaters, which can be adorned with a simple “geometric” piece.

Your achievements in life, financial position and taste are best emphasised by parures — sets of several pieces: earrings and ring, earrings, ring and bracelet, and so on. The main thing is that the pieces match in style and type of stones.

Full parures include a necklace, earrings, bracelet and ring of the same design. They should never all be worn at once. Wear the necklace with the ring, and the earrings with the bracelet. A wedding ring can be worn with them.

Today sets with coloured stones combined with diamonds and pearls are especially fashionable. Jewellery with the classic precious stones — rubies, sapphires, emeralds, diamonds — has never gone out of fashion.

The choice of engagement and wedding rings deserves special attention. Often these are the only jewellery a woman has all her life. Today many brides firmly decline diamond rings. If you do intend to wear one, take part in choosing the size of the stone, and when deciding, consider how comfortable a ring with a large stone will be to wear and whether a ring with small but beautiful stones might be preferable. It is commonly held that the groom must buy the most expensive ring he can afford. But would a bride — often a modest working woman — really want her future husband to go into debt?

Incidentally, rather than a shop-bought ring, it is far more touching to receive from one’s beloved his mother’s or grandmother’s ring — this undoubtedly says that not only the young man but his whole family take the coming marriage seriously and warmly.

Men’s jewellery

All men’s jewellery should be made of precious metals, simple in design and solid in workmanship. Never reject things for being “old-fashioned”. In the West many men wear antique signet rings or tie pins with a large diamond as a symbol of their status, and magnificent craftsmanship removes any objection to them.

A signet ring looks better on a man’s hand with long fingers than on a plump, short-fingered one. An antique gold signet ring with a coat of arms or monogram can be worn on the little finger of either hand. A ring with an emblem — a Masonic one, for example — is worn emblem inward. A ring on the index or middle finger is a showy or theatrical manner.

Watches and cigarette cases should be gold or silver, steel or platinum, and may be enamelled.

A wristwatch may be worn with evening dress if it is on a leather strap. Remember the golden rule: no excess. Better to look a little more modest than to be known as someone who knows no measure in clothes or jewellery.

Children’s jewellery

Stores today offer a wide variety of children’s jewellery, varying both in price and in materials: yellow and white gold, silver and coloured enamels are widely used. Adults buy babies and teenagers all kinds of presents for birthdays and other occasions, from traditional bracelets and pendants to charming toys in precious metals.

Teenage girls certainly love jewellery, and today’s designers create a whole range for every taste, from classic to unusual and extravagant.

Jewellery in precious materials is a wonderful graduation present.

Studying demand for children’s jewellery, specialists have concluded that the main criterion for parents remains the brand. Most adults believe one should start with well-known brands that can shape children’s taste and style of life.

Old advice on how to wear jewellery

Very relevant advice was given to young girls and married women in the 19th century. From childhood, lessons in good manners taught that a young girl should not wear diamonds until her wedding. A few strands of pearls, coral or turquoise earrings in gold or silver, a locket or cross on a black velvet ribbon, or a gold watch on a fine chain — that was all a girl could allow herself before marriage.

Girls’ rings were usually small and simple, worn on the ring finger of the left hand.

With the status of a married lady, a woman gained the right to wear jewellery with precious stones. But after thirty her love of jewellery was supposed to be restrained, and her pieces were to have exceptional artistic merit. In the daytime one wore coloured stones, massive gold pieces and cameos. Diamonds were considered appropriate only in the evening; a diamond necklace was worn only for a grand occasion — a ball or a theatre premiere — and only with a low-cut dress. A diamond necklace goes with any other jewellery, yet a girl from high society was taught from an early age to be extremely restrained in choosing it. Not everyone admired diamonds, however. Ernst Brücke, in his Physiology of Colour, remarked that diamonds, with their harsh glitter, beautify no face but make it older and plainer, and wondered how long ladies would go on wearing them if they cost no more than a peasant woman’s glass beads. Diamonds have long been valued chiefly as family heirlooms, an investment and a symbol of family solidity.

Jewellery was to be worn at the right place and time. Diamonds appropriate at a society reception were unacceptable for a daytime walk; it was generally considered improper to wear sparkling jewellery in the daytime. As for “false” stones — paste — they could be worn by ladies who for various reasons had lost the family jewels. If ladies fallen on hard times refused to part with their diamonds even when they could not feed their own children, society condemned them as vain upstarts.

The rules for matching jewels to the colour of a dress were just as strict. Pearls, diamonds and gold were held to harmonise with any colour. Coral, however, was to be worn only with a black, white or grey dress; on coloured clothes it was considered tasteless. Amethysts were never worn with blue, light blue or lilac, rubies with red, turquoise with blue or green. A red-haired beauty would never wear red stones but would prefer emerald, chrysoprase and other green stones. Blondes were advised to wear sapphires and turquoise. Young girls could afford only modest earrings with pearls or aquamarines.

All these rules may dismay our contemporaries, especially women, yet it is hard to deny the good sense of those who made them. So it is worth heeding a few more recommendations from etiquette books of the time. One of them marvels at a lady wearing a heavy gold chain resembling the iron chains used to restrain animals — costly, no doubt, and thick enough to hold a calf — and calls such a perversion of taste a sign of the owner’s lack of cultivation: a lady should not wear a piece merely because it is expensive or because fashion demands some clumsy form, nor choose a thing thoughtlessly, looking only at its price.

The same book advises on earrings: ladies with unattractive ears should not wear them (nor should those with asymmetrical faces), and those with quick, angular movements are advised against dangling earrings, which make an unpleasant impression on others with every sharp movement. A woman with a short neck should beware of very long drops: touching the shoulders, they lose their grace.

Jewellery to suit your lifestyle

When choosing a piece — and especially the setting of its stones — consider your personality and your way of life. Be realistic and remember that there is no piece of jewellery that cannot break. Even a diamond can split from a sharp accidental blow. Classic four- or six-prong settings suit people who are not very active, but they may not suit a modern woman. A bezel setting, by contrast, does not reduce the stone’s brilliance and protects it better.

People who lead an active life and do a lot of sport are advised not to buy rings with marquise or pear-cut stones: such stones have points that are easily damaged by unexpected knocks. Moreover, a ring’s shank and prongs wear and lose their lustre over time if you garden, play on the beach, climb mountains, ski or cycle. If you swim regularly in a chlorinated pool, remember to take off your jewellery, because chlorine corrodes metal at solder joints and bends.

A word on ring design. Rings are usually round, but fingers are not. Top-heavy rings will turn on the finger unless they have square or horseshoe-shaped contours that fit the finger. Remember too that rings worn on neighbouring fingers rub against each other and wear faster.

Jewellery should be chosen to match or contrast with clothing. In each case consider the silhouette and purpose of the clothes, their colour and cut, your age, height, face shape and features, the colour of your hair, eyes and skin, and your hairstyle. Jewellery should not only complement an outfit but shape your image.

How to choose a bracelet

Specialists identify several classic bracelet forms: round bangles, open cuffs, charm bracelets and chains with an engraved plate.

Round bangles have been known since time immemorial; in the Middle Ages they were a symbol of prosperity. The main requirement today is lightness, so the piece cannot harm the wrist. Round bracelets, with or without a clasp, are worn singly or several at once.

The first to wear an open cuff are thought to have been the Incas. The absence of a clasp makes it easy to put on and take off, but remember that such a bracelet can easily be bent out of shape.

People have decorated bracelets with charms and talismans since ancient times; such bracelets were believed to bring luck and ward off evil.

The chain bracelet with an engraved plate came into civilian life from the army, where the plate carried a soldier’s basic details. Today it is engraved with a loved one’s name or an aphorism meaningful to the owner.

Recently the “tennis bracelet” has become common: a classic round bracelet, often set with small diamonds, more rarely sapphires. Tennis bracelets in various materials, techniques and prices are in great demand today.

The latest fashion is to wear a bracelet with a watch on the same arm. Sets of spring-shaped bracelet-rings in yellow and rose gold are especially fashionable.

How do you choose a bracelet that will be comfortable to wear?

First, when trying on a bracelet, make sure it fits — neither too long nor too short. Remember the main rule: the gap between bracelet and wrist should equal the width of one middle finger.

Second, check carefully that all the links are identical and fit together precisely. If the gap between links is larger anywhere, the line of the bracelet will soon be broken and kinks may appear there. Rough spots and small burrs on the links also indicate poor quality.

Third, look closely at the clasp — usually the weakest point. Make sure it works well and that its tongue has no cracks or other damage.

And to be sure the clasp will never let you down, choose bracelets with additional safety features. The most common today are an extra safety chain or a rigid figure-of-eight catch.

Which ring to buy?

The choice of a ring rests on two factors: the structure of the hand and the owner’s age.

A young girl is best suited by thin, delicate rings of simple design: they emphasise the tenderness of young skin and the neat proportions of a beautiful hand. On an older woman’s hand a small ring draws attention not to the jewel but to the hand itself, making the finger joints more noticeable. A large ring with a big stone lends an older woman’s hand elegance and adorns it.

Women’s hands with long, slender fingers are beautifully suited by horizontally designed rings — massive or stacked rings look wonderful. If a woman has short fingers, a vertically designed ring will visually lengthen the hand. But then it is better to avoid wide rings that cover the space between two joints and make the fingers look shorter.

Hands with knotty joints, and “masculine” hands, should be adorned with wide rings set with large gemstones: such rings visually reduce the hand. Plump fingers look more graceful with an asymmetric ring; triangular forms are good too. Asymmetry and angles conceal plumpness.

Choosing a necklace

Young buyers are suited by short, fine chains at the throat or strands of small pearls, which emphasise the beauty and grace of a smooth young neck.

If age needs to be disguised, long chains and necklaces (46–60 cm) with large stones are best. Long necklaces of stones larger than 7 mm lengthen the neck and frame the face and hairstyle.

A short neck is visually lengthened by chains and necklaces lying 5 cm below the collarbone.

Short pieces emphasise the length of the neck. A strong (thick) neck can be lengthened by large chains and necklaces, whereas short pieces draw attention to its thickness.

Women with a long, slender neck should definitely avoid Y-shaped chains, which visually lengthen an already long neck.

Matching jewellery to your style of dress

When choosing jewellery, look at it critically. The word “criticism” comes from the Greek for “to judge”.

A critical assessment of a piece is based on analysis. Designs are analysed by a number of closely related criteria, and only after analysing all of them is it advisable to buy. Of course you can buy a piece simply because you like it, but unfortunately disappointment may follow some time later.

So if you have decided to buy a piece of jewellery, take the trouble, before paying, to answer a few questions:

  1. Is it for every day or for special occasions? What matters more to you now — wearing elegant earrings to work every day, or adorning yourself with an unusual necklace once a year, on New Year’s Eve, say?
  2. Does the piece match your lifestyle and image? Does it emphasise your strengths and help conceal flaws in your appearance?
  3. Is the piece comfortable? Do the setting of the stones and the construction suit your way of life?
  4. Do the artistic image of the piece and its stones make you stand out from the crowd?
  5. Which outfits will you wear it with?
  6. What are its technical execution and construction quality like? Is it free of defects; does it meet international quality standards?
  7. Will it be easy to care for?

Lifestyle and style of dress are a single whole. Jewellery has always been the main accessory to any clothing in any fabric, further emphasising a woman’s style and image.

Here are a few recommendations on combining fabrics and jewellery.

Clothes in “predatory” prints — leopard, snakeskin — almost never go out of fashion. They are well complemented by jewellery of simple, so-called primitive forms with large stones, and by belts with heavy chains and brooch-buckles.

Varied metal surfaces and textures are in fashion — for example unpolished gold combined with chased gold. Such pieces go well with both dressy suits and dresses and sportswear.

Rough natural nuggets have become very fashionable in jewellery; such pieces go well with knitwear.

For Tiffany in New York, designers created a whole collection of jewellery in the form of stylised insects set with coloured stones. Such pieces further emphasise the chic of a natural silk dress.

When it comes to the colours of stones, one distinguishes dark and light, cool and warm, spring and autumn colours. The difference between “dark” and “light” is not only visual but emotional. The emotions arise from our associations: the form of the piece, the colour of its stones and the shape and style of cut evoke feelings of rest, movement, rising, falling and so on. Lines and geometric shapes affect us emotionally: a horizontal line is always associated with rest; a vertical one makes us think of striving upward; an oblique line suggests instability and falling; a wavy line suggests movement and rotation.

Using the varied qualities of gemstones, metals and fabrics in combining clothing and jewellery gives a woman boundless opportunities to express her taste and individuality.

When we choose a piece for a particular outfit, the first visual impression is determined by the outfit’s silhouette and contours, which also determine the form of the piece. The overall size of a form appears larger or smaller depending on the contrast of large and small within it: small details in a large form emphasise its size.

Seeing a piece as a harmonious three-dimensional form for a particular suit or dress, consider the decorative properties of the metals and alloys it is made of, which minerals are set in it, and whether enamel, niello or other materials were used.

The properties of the materials are judged by appearance: texture, colour, surface pattern.

Texture means the character of the surface. Parts of a piece may be polished, matt, engraved and so on; niello or multicoloured enamels also enhance texture. Surface finish matters greatly in choosing clothes: matt and textured pieces go better with silk, while a tweed suit looks more luxurious with a highly polished piece.

One of the main conditions for combining clothes and jewellery is unity — consistency and subordination of the one to the other.

There are several ways to harmonise clothing and jewellery: identity, nuance and contrast.

The calmest and safest choice is identity — complete similarity of style between clothing and jewellery (for example, a dark classic suit with classic jewellery: pearl beads, earrings or a diamond ring).

Nuance is a relationship between jewellery and clothing of similar style that approach each other in their features. Similarity is stronger than difference, but there is always at least one element that stands out — a ring with a very large coloured stone of unusual cut, for example.

Contrast is a clear inequality, an opposition between the style of clothing and of jewellery: fabrics, metals and designs are set against each other. This is the boldest choice and will certainly emphasise your individuality — but a sense of proportion is needed here too.

Contrast between clothing and jewellery can be created not only by the shape of the piece against the lines of the clothes but also by combining two contrasting colours.

How to attract attention with jewellery

For your image to be memorable and individual, jewellery is essential. Several principles help draw attention to your jewellery:

  1. The law of quantity. Other things being equal, the woman wearing more jewellery will dominate any company. Large stones, voluminous pieces and richly decorated jewellery are always remembered; with such pieces you will always stand out and dominate your surroundings.
  2. The law of central placement. A very large stone or a piece of unusual design placed relatively centrally (in a necklace or brooch) singles a woman out, other things being equal.
  3. The law of quality. The woman whose jewellery is set with very high-quality stones dominates.
  4. The law of meaning. Jewellery whose decoration carries meaning dominates over jewellery with purely formal decoration.

An example of style and individuality is Audrey Hepburn’s look in Breakfast at Tiffany’s (a black Givenchy dress, a string of pearls, long gloves and an upswept hairstyle), which remains one of the great “style icons”, as its frequent use in advertising shows. Thanks to the film, business at Tiffany & Co. took off sharply.

Talking to customers today

It used to be thought that a conversation with a customer begins at the counter and ends at the till. A good salesperson knew how to close the deal, and remembered regulars by sight. That is still valuable, but the conversation has long left the store: it starts in the app, continues in a messenger and does not end after the purchase.

ThenNow
Contact only in the storeChat, video consultation, phone and store form one continuous conversation
The salesperson remembers facesClienteling: purchase history, sizes and important dates in the CRM
Push for the saleHelp choose without pressure: pressure kills the repeat purchase
One script for everyoneA personal approach: occasion, taste, budget, who it is for
Opinions belong to the salespersonOpinions live in reviews, answered publicly and to the point

Who buys. Customers are changing. According to SUNLIGHT, its main audience is women, while men mostly buy gifts. Younger customers increasingly buy jewellery for themselves, without an occasion and on impulse, while older ones see gold and diamonds as an investment. Each group needs its own tone and arguments.

Clienteling. A personal consultant looks after “their” customers: they know the ring size, the favourite metal, birthdays and anniversaries. A week before an important date they write: “We have picked three options for your anniversary — shall we set them aside?” This top level of service used to exist only in expensive boutiques; CRM systems and messengers now make it possible everywhere.

Video consultation. Show the piece on the consultant’s hand in daylight, next to a coin for scale, in motion. For expensive purchases this is almost like a store visit.

Tone. Polite, warm and simple, without stock phrases. Write briefly: one message, one idea. Do not send mailings to people who have opted out. Respect for the customer’s time and boundaries is the new politeness.

What still holds. Good advice starts with questions, and customers perceive jewellery emotionally. The consultant’s task is still the same: to help a person picture the piece in their own life.
