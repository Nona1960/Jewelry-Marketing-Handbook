Goals and tasks of the head of a jewellery company

A leader in the jewellery market needs a quality that might be called emotional wisdom. It shows itself as the ability to accept people as they are; to approach things on the basis of the specific situation; to treat everyone, even close acquaintances, with courteous attention; to trust others even when it seems risky; and to do without constant approval and recognition.

The head of a jewellery business must be able to:

  • represent the organisation at meetings — they are the face of the company;
  • motivate staff to act, consolidating their efforts;
  • maintain contacts with partners;
  • monitor the flow of information;
  • share information with staff;
  • pass information outside the firm;
  • initiate and plan change;
  • find a way out of unusual situations;
  • handle staffing and pay;
  • negotiate.

As the Canadian scholar Henry Mintzberg writes in The Nature of Managerial Work, the ideal manager:

  • does a great deal of work at an unrelenting pace;
  • deals with tasks marked by variety, brevity and fragmentation;
  • prefers current, specific and non-routine issues;
  • prefers oral communication to written;
  • works through a network of internal and international contacts;
  • faces many constraints, yet can influence how the firm works.

American management research has shown that the ideal leader should be:

  • firm, yet flexible;
  • serious, yet with a sense of humour;
  • friendly, yet able to keep their distance;
  • devoted to the business, yet not losing their head over success or defeat;
  • a dreamer, yet with a strong will.

Several lessons (principles) can be drawn from the careers of successful business people:

  1. Go with the flow. Only the most pragmatic and accomplished “chameleons” survive on the battlefields of business.
  2. No charity. There is no place for “nice guys” in big business; “good guys” always come second. Tough pragmatists survive in the business world. Being a nice person is no reason to be trusted; trust is earned by strength of character and competence. The end result matters more than doubts about how it was achieved.
  3. Place your bets. It is important to be a player. You have to take risks and win. The bigger the stakes, the more noise — and the more profit.
  4. Be a leader. Brush aside all talk of dividing up authority: the company must be led. Stay close to the corridors of power. Politicians always change the situation — for better or worse — and their decisions affect business. If possible, get involved in their decision-making. Leave politics to the politicians, but make sure they leave your business to you.
  5. King of details. You must know your business from every side. Base your deals on intuition rather than on some grand idea or philosophy.
  6. Marketing in the blood. Selling to the customer should be an instinct, an automatic impulse.
  7. As if on doping. While your competitors are pondering their next decision, be the first to pick up the phone and make the deal. Slowness is the lot of losers; only the fastest survive. Keep renewing and developing your network of contacts. If your contacts are outdated and useless, you are outdated and useless too.
  8. Prepare your sledge in summer. If you want to succeed in the future, think everything through in advance. Do not focus too much on any single deal: today’s top priority may be irrelevant tomorrow.
  9. Lifelong ambition. You should never have enough of the new. Back the winners, not the losers.
  10. Don’t stop. Constant activity is the sure road to success. If you live in yesterday, you are dead. Keep learning and working. Old dogs not only can but must learn new tricks. Keep asking questions whenever you see something new.

Besides, the leader, more than anyone, must show good manners. If you want your staff to respect you, try to treat everyone evenly. A boss should never criticise or reproach a subordinate in front of other employees. When the head enters a department, they always greet people first. Let a woman enter the lift first, go ahead on the stairs, help her with the door, and so on.

It is important for the leader of a jewellery business to have charisma. Charisma is a mysterious power that arouses rapturous admiration in others. It springs from some inner quality — self-belief, sexual energy, determination — that most people lack but dream of. Charisma is plainly visible; it overflows and permeates the leader’s whole being. A charismatic person is extremely vivid, with an unusual, magnetic presence. Charismatic people do not hide their need for love and admiration. They are open to their audience and genuinely need it, feeding on its energy. There is also a streak of adventure and risk in them, very attractive to those who find life dull. In every case, show reckless daring and courage — let people see you take risks for the good of others. Show heroism: it makes the aura shine even brighter. Conversely, the slightest hint of cowardice will irretrievably destroy your charisma, however powerful it was.

The leader’s energy plays the main role in moving others to act and in releasing the inner energy of the team. Leaders draw energy from everyday affairs, from daily routine. They generate ideas that kindle enthusiasm. They invent new approaches and keep trying new methods. Enthusiasm is what a leader values endlessly and constantly cultivates.

Successful business people, whatever they read or are told, as a rule believe in nothing. They are corporate atheists. Their god is profit or ego. If you need proof, read the autobiographies of successful dealmakers, men or women alike: book-length self-admiration, usually driven by greed or the urge to assert oneself (or both).

Here are a few descriptions of the life values of jewellery-market leaders. Stefan Hafner cannot stand the empty, pretty words that usually accompany the jewellery business. He dislikes it when what he does is described in vague terms such as “dream”, “sparkle”, “luxury”. In his view, you need to watch not only nature but also the stock-market news — then you know what is happening in the world. Next, you need a business approach to jewellery: what you make must be appealing and look expensive while costing little — the maximum for the minimum money. And that means you need air, empty space.

Silvia Damiani recalls how her father began to involve her in the business: she was 22 when she went with him to Japan to buy pearls. He told her then that the business needed three qualities: instinct, to understand the human soul and offer it something of your own; audacity, so as not to be daunted by difficulties; and heart, to know when to stop — by heart he meant the capacity to feel and to care. There is no negotiation without mediation, and if you have instinct, audacity and heart, you will be able to assess the situation and manage it without ever letting things come to discord or a break.

Is there such a thing as an ideal leader?

As for staff, here is what I wanted to say. Of course human qualities matter, no question. But they are not the priority for today’s tasks. You will not find the ideal employee — they do not exist. Only a fool looks for one person who will solve every problem. A director today is a team. Every director — you, for example — has people reporting to them. Our task now is to make traffic not three million times twenty but ten million times three, you see. And you have to find the people who will do it. It is all very simple: staffing should be solved month by month. You brought in one successful person who does the job — good. The company needs change, and change will not happen without losses and conflicts. What has betrayal got to do with it? That is naivety and amateurism in management. It means the manager is still weak. I like the books of Adizes — there is such an author. He says the ideal manager is a myth; there is no such person.

The main mistakes that lead to failure

The main mistakes that lead to failure are arrogance, greed, ignorance, wishful thinking, loss of focus, pride, and hiring friends. Let us look at each of them.

  1. Arrogance. Success often leads to arrogance, and arrogance to failure. Conceit is the main enemy of successful marketing; what is needed is objectivity. When people succeed, they tend to lose an objective view of things. They even allow themselves to look down on what the market needs. The best leaders of jewellery businesses can think the way consumers think. They put themselves in the customer’s place and never rely on their own opinion of how the consumer ought to behave in a given situation. A number of studies show that after a long period of success people stop making the best business decisions.
  2. Greed. This quality is like a virus spreading among an organisation’s top managers. Seeing those around you make millions, you start thinking you should do the same. Soon you are making decisions with your own profit in mind, which is not always good for the company’s net profit.
  3. Ignorance. Most large companies get into trouble because they do not understand that strategy is a battle for the minds of existing and potential customers. It is not about the better product; it is about the better perception of the product.
  4. Wishful thinking. This happens when a company plans on the basis of dreams rather than facts. The question is not what you want to do but what you can do.
  5. Loss of focus. Instead of sticking to their business, company directors lose interest in it and fall victim to dreams of new lines of activity.
  6. Pride. A sense of absolute certainty that you are right because your company holds a solid market position. Your organisation is bigger, richer and, as it seems to you, knows more than anyone, so you start underestimating your competitors. Instead of admitting your mistakes — which are quite possible — you think anyone can be wrong except you.
  7. Hiring friends. This is a big mistake. Often the problem is that you do not know your friends nearly as well as you think. Friends sometimes agree with you just to avoid a quarrel. They carefully hide their less attractive traits and laugh loudly at your jokes. Since honesty rarely strengthens friendship, you may never learn what your friends really feel. Another problem with hiring friends is that friendship inevitably limits your authority. A friend is rarely the person best able to help you. In the end, knowledge and competence matter far more than friendly feelings. Every working situation requires a certain distance between people.

And now some live remarks on leadership from an interview with Sergey Gribnyakov:

– Sergey Anatolyevich, let’s start with arrogance. When you talk with your staff, business partners or friends, how do you feel about being talked down to? Perhaps you have had such experiences with foreign business people — someone was haughty, while someone else knew how to draw you into a friendly conversation…

– Many examples come to mind. A Russian person may both tolerate rude, domineering treatment and behave that way towards others. Evidently in the Soviet period most people’s pride was broken, and so some allow themselves to be arrogant, to show how much power they have. You know, a prison governor might behave like the master of the place. Many of us are like that. In China, though, when you are closing a business deal, if you show the habits of a big boss it is perceived as something wild and unbearable. In mainland China or Hong Kong, a person who puts themselves above their station and is unfriendly, inflexible and full of pride may be seen simply as a madman, although nobody will show it. The English influence on local culture and upbringing still tells: the Chinese do not express emotions openly. In negotiations I sense intuitively that there you must never allow yourself even a hint of being the boss or a haughty person. That is probably the most vivid example from my own experience.

Is there a line between greed and thrift in business?

– Greed is a quality like a virus that spreads among the top managers of organisations. Seeing those around you earn millions, you start thinking you should do the same. And soon you are making business decisions with your own profit in mind, which is not always good for your company’s net profit. How do you feel about greed? Have you come across examples of it that seemed vile or ridiculous?

– Yes, this is a sore point of Russian education in general, especially in business. Which case should I tell? About ten years ago I interviewed a Russian manager from the French company Auchan. He laughed at its owners for setting such a low price on cheap socks: you could sell a pair for at least 110 roubles instead of, say, 45, but the French were so stupid, they always kept the average receipt down… I noted to myself then how primitively some managers think: make the product more expensive and earn more. In short, such companies lose their face. In the end the consumer may not see what distinguishes a company if all manufacturers and sellers act the same way. But that is not my point. My point is that deriving a formula for success from classical education, based on profit, is foolish. You must base it solely on satisfying the customer’s needs and interests. As soon as greed appears, it turns into a different formula, where profit comes first — and profit gives no competitive advantage and ends in fiasco.

– Now about wishful thinking. It happens when a company plans on the basis of dreams rather than facts. The question is not what you want to do but what you can do. Did your partners often take their wishes for reality?

– Well, I probably suffer from it myself, so why should I judge my partners… That is my problem too — taking wishes for reality. Of course I would like my products to be loved more, because they deserve more attention. But, you see, it isn’t happening yet. Something must be missing, some skills.

– Loss of focus in business shows itself when, instead of continuing their business, company directors lose interest in it and fall victim to dreams of new activities.

– New activities carry maximum risk. The flip side of the question is defining your niche, the one where you are most competitive. It also matters how far that niche can scale, given your competences and ambitions. If it scales poorly, however talented you are, its boundaries are hard to push. But if you have found a niche with growth coefficients — arithmetic, geometric, compound interest — if that is possible as the business develops, then of course that is the priority. You should abandon a business only when it has reached a dead end. I don’t know, it is a very difficult question… You should only do what you know how to do or have mastered. Changing niche is a risky story; better not. And if the old and new fields are very different from each other, then definitely not.

– Most large companies get into trouble because they fail to understand that strategy is a battle for the minds of existing and potential customers. It is not about the better product — it is about the better perception of the product.

– It is probably a sum of factors; one does not contradict the other. But you should not count on a manufacturer or seller being able to push a worse product through marketing. A small difference in quality between your product and its rivals can be magnified and highlighted by advertising, but promotion cannot help a product that is worse — that would be money thrown away.

– And what about hiring friends?

– Such relationships always demand a lot of attention from the host side, so to speak. People are collective creatures, and if you don’t begrudge the time, you can work with friends. But they will never appreciate the sacrifices you make for them.

What should top management prioritise?

I think today the phone comes first: social media, video content, media, information, the shaping of human consciousness. I like that the SUNLIGHT Media Group is headed by Nona Dronova. She is the editor-in-chief of the most progressive area today, studying Yandex.Zen, systems, reach, impressions, volumes, costs, the audience and what it needs. I think that is progress!

We are now actively redesigning our stores. We are launching a new logo and corporate identity. This must be used in our communications.

  1. We must get across to the consumer that SUNLIGHT is now different — that SUNLIGHT is not a jewellery brand (cheap, from China). SUNLIGHT is an online hypermarket that brings together manufacturers and brands from Russia and the whole world. It is important to form a clear understanding of what we are in the consumer’s eyes. We should probably come up with a story explaining why the changes happened (a merger with a Russian manufacturer, new shareholders, a new business strategy…).
  2. The website needs to be reviewed. It should carry more information about specific manufacturers — in Russia, in China and so on — with photos, videos, real people, interviews…
  3. We definitely need designers: real people with real collections. Collections should become seasonal; then, over time, they can logically be marked down. We should bring in both unknown designers and world-famous names — we need a good reputation, status. It doesn’t cost huge money, and the company can grow powerful.

I believe we must launch an advertising campaign on the theme “SUNLIGHT is different now”. A question for you: when should we do it, given the holidays?

And one more thing: a rebranding like this is a good news hook for a sale. It is almost like a store closing, only a closing of the whole chain — clearing everything out before the renewal.

Summary and exercises

Key points

  • The head of a jewellery company is responsible not only for profit but for the goal the whole team is moving towards; every employee must understand it.
  • There is no ideal leader: the owner’s strengths need to be complemented by people with different qualities.
  • Typical causes of failure are losing control over money and stock, emotional decisions, and unwillingness to learn and change with the market.
  • What separates thrift from greed is the time horizon: savings that undermine people, quality or reputation cost more in the end.

Review questions

  2. How does sensible economy differ from greed? Give an example from jewellery retail.
  3. Which management mistakes most often bring a jewellery business down?
  4. What should top management prioritise during a period of rapid growth?
