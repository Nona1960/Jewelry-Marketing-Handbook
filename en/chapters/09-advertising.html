Advertising in the jewellery market

Advertising is showing your product when you cannot meet the consumer in person. A TV commercial or a print ad works instead of you.

You want people to buy your product and not someone else’s? Then every advertising campaign must begin by showing how your product differs from others and by stressing the benefits that follow from those differences.

The main aim of advertising is to persuade people to buy your products. So you must convince potential customers that your product is the best and that they simply need it.

There are two methods of persuasion:

  1. By argument. This requires the ability to distinguish facts from opinion. Facts can be measured, studied and checked for reliability; an opinion is a point of view or an assessment of a situation.
  2. By justification. This is an appeal to the customer to satisfy their human needs and desires, which can be grouped by motive: self-preservation, economic security, social recognition, aesthetics, and giving pleasure to loved ones.

To persuade by justification, you must make the potential customer feel the need to satisfy a specific motive and offer the means to do so. You can:

  • list the benefits the customer will get from buying your product;
  • paint a picture of their personal prospects: reciprocity, respect, a good mood.

Analysing motivation with arguments for and against will show how thoroughly you have prepared and spare you objections from potential customers. But studying the audience or market niche is crucial if you want your campaign to succeed.

The old style of market segmentation no longer meets today’s needs. Modern segmentation takes into account the gradual evolution of buyer psychology based on individual needs. Until you find out what people want and why, you cannot divide the market clearly and correctly in a way that helps sell your goods.

Turning to psychology is a crucial step. Most important is to understand the key motives behind people’s actions — what moves them and drives them to act. This is needed to develop the right approach to the consumer. Taking lifestyle into account helps pinpoint coming changes in consumer psychology or find out what exactly gives them satisfaction.

Before launching a campaign, ask yourself:

  • Why should people be interested in what we are advertising?
  • What do they think of our product? Can it be improved?
  • What moods prevail in society?

Three generally recognised factors make the source of an advertising message credible: expertise, trustworthiness and attractiveness.

Expertise here is the degree of competence the potential buyer attributes to the source in backing up its claims.

Trustworthiness indicates how objective and honest the source is perceived to be.

Attractiveness is the favourable impression the advertising makes on the audience. It is increased by qualities such as candour, humour and naturalness. So the most credible source is the one that scores highest on all three.

When deciding on an advertising campaign, the entrepreneur must answer the following questions:

  1. What goals are set for the advertising?
  2. How do they relate to the firm’s goals?
  3. How much money is allocated to advertising?
  4. Which mass media are used in advertising, and how?
  5. How do customers rate the firm’s forms of advertising?
  6. On what basis does the advertising department choose its media?
  7. Is there a link between more active advertising and changes in jewellery sales?
  8. Does the advertising have a corporate style?

The view has recently taken hold that jewellery “wins” much more when it appears in magazines or on TV not on its own but in the context of a “lifestyle”. The idea is that whatever situation a person is in, the jewellery that creates their individual style is part of their way of life. Developing this concept, specialists at world-famous firms began actively using celebrities in jewellery advertising. Some specialists believe a company’s image can be strengthened by placing the portrait of its head on advertising posters. This supposedly removes the impersonality of the “manufacturer–buyer” relationship and adds a certain trust and humanity. Such advertising can be placed not only in print.

Interviews in magazines or on TV with famous people wearing particular jewellery make a good impression. A woman sees a new piece on her favourite star and starts longing to have the same. Photographs of Jacqueline Kennedy in a women’s magazine, for example, significantly boosted sales of Van Cleef emerald rings, Cartier watches and Tiffany enamel bracelets.

Jewellery companies increasingly use pop stars in their advertising. A singer popular in Japan, for instance, “speaks” to his female fans from an advertising page: “Just for you” — and the bracelet box also contains his new album.

Remember that a large-scale advertising campaign is not enough to build a strong brand. In developing an image strategy, do not forget a few other techniques:

  1. Giving products away. Distributing promotional samples is the basis of the image of companies specialising in mass-market goods.
  2. Public relations. Giving away samples is wonderful but useless if nobody knows about it. You might start with advertising in the mass press and then share management experience in the business press.

One important marketing activity is shaping public opinion about the jewellery firm and its products. Successful public relations requires:

  • systematically building a favourable attitude towards the firm among the general public, including government bodies and state institutions — maintaining contacts with the media through press conferences, articles in newspapers and magazines, public or charitable activities, anniversary events and open days;
  • trade presentations showing jewellery, its merits, existing models, variants and prices;
  • prestige corporate advertising that supports the firm’s image;
  • advisory services, handing out materials about gemstones, fashion trends and so on.

A few general tips on organising an advertising campaign:

  1. For your advertising to succeed, enlist specialists. Listen to agencies that not only know how to promote a product but have experience of doing so.
  2. Your marketing events should be bright and creative, more like shows than business events. Choose the best venues for them.
  3. Use the popularity of your business partners. Using your brand together with other well-known brands helps it spread widely and become known to the public. Not every brand will do, though: co-promote only with companies that will increase trust in your firm, expand your market presence and sales or take you into new markets.
  4. It is good if your company’s advertising can be seen everywhere: on the streets and in taxis, on TV and radio.
  5. Broadcast media are better than print, so where possible choose radio and television — and exhibitions. Magazines, newspapers and direct mail are less effective.

Travelling around the world, you notice that advertising in one country is unlike advertising in another, though the brands are mostly the same. In planning a campaign it is very important to understand how people in your particular country will perceive it.

Western marketers have studied the effectiveness of investment by type of advertising: directive, associative, celebrity and shock advertising. “Directive” meant an instruction or command; “associative” meant a link of ideas whereby one idea, appearing in the mind, calls up another by similarity; “shock” meant embarrassing people by breaking accepted norms of decency; and “celebrity” meant someone famous and renowned.

The main mistakes in creating advertising are:

  • no idea;
  • inability to use images and symbols;
  • judging quality by the principle “I like it, so others will too”.

Expect nothing from advertising that appears only now and then. For advertising to affect consumers, the company should be mentioned in at least three or four consecutive media issues. Effectiveness depends on memorability, and how deeply a message penetrates the conscious and subconscious mind depends directly on how often it is repeated. Advertising must be remembered, so choose the colour, style, slogan, logo and character once and do not change them without real need. Learn one rule: advertising is creativity within strict forms.

Jewellery companies’ advertising modules in magazines

A few tips for designing a successful advertising module:

  1. Decide what you are going to promote: the company, a product line, services, a single piece.
  2. Decide who the advertising is aimed at — manufacturers, independent jewellers, trading houses, retailers, end customers. This determines which publication you choose and what the text will be.
  3. Always consider the level of your message — logical or emotional. People take in 90% of information emotionally.
  4. Be careful with bright colours; they are not for expensive goods. Jewellery should be presented with restraint.
  5. Choose your words carefully. Be brief. Remove “best”, “most” and the like — they do not attract attention but rather repel it.

Rules worth following when designing a magazine module:

  1. Be brief: information is absorbed only in the first three seconds.
  2. The slogan should contain no more than three or four words.
  3. Use no more than two or three images in the layout.
  4. Text must not be too small: letter height should be at least 1/6 of the height of a horizontal layout and 1/18 of a vertical one.
  5. Do not use hard-to-read typefaces or more than three different typefaces.
  6. Place the logo at the bottom centre or bottom right. It should be large enough to be noticed but in proportion to the module.
  7. The idea should be clear at first glance.
  8. Use no more than three colours so the module does not look too gaudy.
  9. The module should stand out, but not irritate.
  10. Repetition is the most effective means of influence. Keeping and repeating your corporate style lets people recognise your firm at a glance.

Every advertising message is based on the AIDA principle:

  • attention;
  • interest;
  • desire;
  • action (prompting it).

Attracting attention is advertising’s first task. Neglecting this rule is one of the most common mistakes. Attention can be voluntary or involuntary. The first thing that “hooks” is the colour scheme or the character. Advertising colours may be irritating and unpleasant and still do their job by triggering subconscious associations.

How do you arouse interest in a potential buyer? Only by stimulating positive emotions and a good mood. For example, the buyer should remember not the “star” but the star wearing your company’s jewellery. The slogan and a message not overloaded with information also play an important role.

Modern consumers buy a product only when they find an emotional connection between it and themselves, so aim for customers to see the company emotionally rather than rationally. For example, offer conditions for mentally making the ideal purchase and show the possibilities the customer will have after it. A girl buys not a necklace but a dream of beauty; a groom buys not a wedding ring but hope.

But creating an attractive, interesting, intriguing module is not everything. If it is not addressed to your target audience, it will attract random visitors but will never raise sales or create a positive image. If your customer is a professional, they will be more interested in the assortment and the characteristics of the pieces; how a pretty girl smiles on the cover hardly matters to them. For them, well-organised information is what counts in advertising, and only expected profit or fast turnover will prompt them to buy.

Now about the idea of the advertising module.

There is so-called creative advertising, which delivers the advertising message to the target audience with maximum effect. A creative idea rests on a fresh look at a product long familiar to the public. A creative advertising technique has a story, a quality image and structured text. Creative advertising lets buyers distinguish your firm’s pieces among thousands of similar ones and lets you save on the frequency of advertising messages.

A few original ideas as examples:

  1. A photo of a walnut that even a hammer cannot crack is associated with the very secure setting of diamonds in the advertiser’s jewellery.
  2. A bullet-proof display case, a safe and a guard also reinforce advertising for secure settings for gemstones.
  3. Photos of babies in nappies suggest something very young and new — the ad of a design studio for especially fashionable jewellery.
  4. The sheen of a porcelain doll enhances the sparkle of a hand-polished necklace, and a roulette table suggests treating jewellery as a game.
  5. A woman in a skirt made of gold chains tells of the possibility of choosing chains of any length for bracelets and necklaces.

Of course, your advertising materials will be prepared by a professional designer. But before commissioning and accepting the work, you should know how certain design features may affect your customers. The first thing to decide is what your advertising material should show and why. Use only the features and details essential to your image and avoid useless information. Elaborate lines and loud colours are not recommended in brochures. Remember: this is not a work of art but a business proposal.

There are generally accepted means of visual influence.

Fonts have their own rules. Tightly spaced letters are hard to read; spacious fonts are easier. It is best to use only two fonts. Text set entirely in capitals is harder to read than text in upper and lower case.

To highlight something, use colour rather than capitals or italics. You must understand the psychology of colour and how to apply it in advertising materials. Red, blue and purple type is hard to read under certain lighting. A white background creates glare, and white type on black can do the same. Royal blue is excellent.

For a stronger effect on the audience you can use familiar stereotypes: red means stop, green means go, yellow means give way. These colours can serve as signals.

Warm colours: red, yellow, gold, brown, scarlet, orange. Cool colours: blue, grey, light green, pastels.

By choosing colours correctly you can make a customer feel “warm” or “cold” towards your products.

Fiery yellow is the colour the eye is drawn to first, followed by white and pure red.

Pastel colours are usually considered weak. Do not choose them for an important idea.

Shades and nuances: it is better to use primary colours and their intensified versions. Dull shades create a vague, uncertain mood.

Use no more than three colours at once.

Use contrasting colours.

Use the same colour for the same element on every page of a brochure.

On a dark background use white, light green, pale yellow or light blue.

On a white or grey background use black, red, dark blue or dark green.

Advertising is an important element of marketing: it informs buyers about specific pieces of jewellery and spreads offers, advice and recommendations on buying them.

An essential function of advertising is to individualise a piece of jewellery — to set it apart from the mass of competing pieces by stressing some feature unique to it (high quality, reliability, a moderate price, original design and so on).

Many people are tired of ordinary photographs of beautiful landscapes or girls in advertising. Research shows that images drawn in watercolour, tempera or pencil have recently become more eye-catching. It may even be a simple drawn frame or a collage of coloured paper. A piece of an advertisement torn from a newspaper also looks interesting.

Creating an advertising message means solving three problems: what to say (content), how to say it logically (structure) and how to express the content as a symbol (form).

The content of the message means an appealing motive or theme that should produce the desired response. There are three types of motive: rational, emotional and moral.

Rational motives relate to the audience’s wish for personal gain. They show that the product or service will deliver the promised benefit — for example, the messages of cutting firms demonstrating the high quality of their gemstone cutting.

Emotional motives are meant to awaken positive feelings that justify a purchase. In jewellery advertising these are most often seduction and love.

Moral motives appeal to family values.

A well-chosen structure helps the advertiser draw a clear conclusion or helps the audience draw it. Remember that putting strong arguments at the start of a message grabs attention at once, but attention may weaken considerably by the end.

The following aspects of an advertisement’s structure are distinguished:

  1. Ability to attract attention (how effectively the headline, layout and quality of illustrations draw attention, and how effectively they reach the categories of buyers the message is aimed at).
  2. Strength of emotional impact (how well the main advertising argument is chosen and presented).
  3. Strength of influence on actual behaviour (how convincingly the message invites people to follow its advice and how likely the buyer is to do what it suggests).
  4. Informativeness (how clearly the main argument is stated and how fully the usefulness of the piece for the buyer is shown).
  5. Ability to make people read to the end (how effectively the message holds readers’ attention).

In print advertising you must decide on the headline, the illustration and the colour scheme. To attract attention, advertisers play on novelty and contrast, use striking illustrations, and work with the size and placement of the jewellery, colour and shape.

When accepting a finished project from the designer, ask yourself and them:

  1. Does the title of the brochure match its intended purpose, and does the brochure give full information?
  2. Could colour have strengthened the emphasis on certain points?
  3. Is the brochure easy to read?
  4. Does the project look professional?
  5. Is the brochure well written?
  6. Does the design help the reader take in the information?
  7. Is there anything superfluous?
  8. Were pictures used to aid understanding?
  9. Have the ideas been simplified for better understanding?
  10. Does the brochure have a solid basic structure and coherence?
  11. Is there too much material?
  12. Is the most important information highlighted with colour, type and images?

An analysis of specialist jewellery magazines from 1996 to 2007 leads to several interesting conclusions about the content, structure and form of advertising modules.

  1. Modules of coloured-stone cutting companies stand out for their clear geometric shapes and bright colours. A successful solution is placing a firm’s elongated module along the side of the right-hand page of a trade magazine. The most effective and favourite colour of diamond-cutting firms is a rich blue background.
  2. Firms in the diamond business often state in their modules that they have branches around the world and that their business is international. A world map or an aeroplane that will instantly deliver the stones you want anywhere on the planet often appears. For diamond-cutting firms a large stock also matters, and pictures such as a supermarket trolley overflowing with diamonds reinforce that impression.
  3. Diamond firms with their own production rather than intermediaries often show professional cutting and gemmological tools and rough diamonds.
  4. Modest, laconic two-colour advertising says that the firm does not need to prove its solidity.
  5. Drawings and cartoons that humorously convey a firm’s particular way of working stand out well.
  6. People in the jewellery business are out of the ordinary; they have the charm of adventure and risk. Demonstrations of strength and courage — silhouettes of predators or wildlife photographs — make a good impression in modules.
  7. Many pearl companies’ modules show high-quality pearls, the process of producing cultured pearls or the places in nature where they are harvested.

Advertising for fine Australian pearls held in rough working hands looks very impressive.

To speed up the effect of advertising, you need to make the potential buyer want to use the services on offer regularly and become the firm’s customer-partner.

Advertising for end buyers of jewellery is very different from advertising for professionals. Each person’s personality rests on their own attitudes, values and goals. These shape their lifestyle and therefore their style of consumption.

Understanding the main differences between types of people, it is easy to choose advertising for jewellery that will interest a potential buyer.

People for whom high social status matters most will notice jewellery advertising in a “First Class” section first, since their key concept is prestige. And if a piece is surrounded by already recognised attributes of prestige, their doubts disappear on their own.

Women who try to look unique, unlike anyone else, need to be offered something unusual. Their behaviour is often unpredictable — a kind of one-actor theatre in which the actor is also the audience.

For so-called hedonists, advertising should offer the attributes of a beautiful, comfortable life — for example, a photograph of jewellery among tableware, fruit or pastries.

Women prone to self-doubt need to follow the “style” of people who serve as their role models and carry authority. Magazine pages that place a piece of jewellery next to a photograph of Penélope Cruz, the Eiffel Tower or a fashion-show report are made for them.

There is a certain category of people, sometimes called “dandies”, who are completely uninterested in others’ opinions and never try to please anyone. They are true masters of the art of living. They live for pleasure rather than work, surround themselves with beautiful, elegant things and show complete freedom. The way to reach them through advertising is to let them view everything as a matter of aesthetic choice. An advertisement from a Japanese magazine was aimed precisely at “dandies”.

Advertising modules for women are usually emphatically bright, even exaggeratedly sexual. The point is to show that a woman is dangerous and that a man pursuing her may lose control of himself. Such modules often feature themes that can loosely be called “Seduction”, “Gifts from a loved one”, “Fairy-tale garden”, “Tradition and classics”, “Shadow” and “Eyes”. Here is a brief description of each.

Seduction

Nothing in today’s world gives a woman more power than the ability to be seductive. Most women dream of such power — the ability to draw men, captivate them and make them their captives. But many see attractiveness as something unattainable, given at birth to a chosen few and beyond the reach of most. That is why successful advertising of seductive women wearing jewellery awakens dormant feelings and gives hope.

Gifts from a loved one

Many of us indulge in daydreams in our youth that fade or even shatter over the years. The ideal lovers in advertisements for wedding rings or diamond rings recreate our past fantasies and idealise our lives. Advertising rings for older women works very well too: stories of two mature people who still love each other are well received.

Fairy-tale garden

The real world is harsh and often merciless. Things happen that we cannot control; people close to us think only of themselves and ignore our feelings; time runs on relentlessly. Fortunately, we develop the saving habit of dreaming early. In that other, imaginary, fairy-tale world we have plenty of bright prospects. For such dreamers an advertisement showing a fairy-tale garden is so powerful that they lose touch with reality, and your jewellery starts to seem the most real thing in life.

Grown women are far from naive: it is impossible to grow up in this world fully naive. Yet there is a category of women who try hard to keep a naive look and to some extent succeed. They exaggerate their weakness to evoke sympathy and behave as if they still see the world through the rose-tinted glasses of childhood and adolescence. Mostly this is a consciously worn mask. If your jewellery design is meant for this type of woman, use their psychological traits in your advertising.

Shadow

Thousands of years ago people believed in various gods and spirits, manifestations of divine power. Mysterious, inexplicable images immediately attract attention and influence potential buyers.

Images that are never clearly visible cannot be grasped. This is the shadow. The shadow is also the dark side of a personality, what makes it mysterious. Chase your shadow and it slips away; turn your back on it and it follows you at once. It is no accident that the “shadow” theme is so often used in jewellery advertising.

Eyes

If any physical feature matters especially in seduction, it is the eyes. They betray passion; a gaze holds, unsettles and stirs a storm of emotion in the audience, achieving its result without words or gestures.

Tradition and classics

You can win the attention of jaded women by turning to the cultural past and using antiques in your modules — symbols of luxury and aristocracy associated with a good pedigree.

Advertising for classic jewellery can be supported by a photograph of a ballerina, the company’s founding date or the owner’s castle in Monaco.

A company with a good history and traditions often links its images with the history of the country or city where it was born. Sometimes a single antique object, such as an old picture frame, is enough to convey historical roots.

A company with a prestigious address or located in a traditionally aristocratic place, such as central Geneva or Paris, always mentions this in its advertising materials.

Advertising a jewellery business on television and the Internet

The main task of a TV commercial is to make your product’s difference interesting to the consumer — to “play it up”. People watch television to be entertained and informed, not because they are eager to see your advertisement. TV advertising can use humour, sex — anything, as long as it gives arguments for buying. People are always interested in news, so try to make the information in your ad sound like big news. Watching an ad takes very little time, so it must be simple. One message is better than two or more; simple images are better than complex ones. Where possible, the words should rhyme — that makes them easier to remember. Above all, the message must contain an obvious and very simple idea.

Remember that advertising does not work on people immediately, so the same ad must be repeated for quite a long time.

An important element of TV advertising is offers, advice and recommendations on buying the jewellery.

Today it pays to advertise on the Internet as well as on TV and radio.

Website design plays an important role. A site must have general information, interesting graphics and a convenient search.

No amount of money will save a bad idea. For a company to grow, it must sell what people will buy. Novelty may help at first, but if a product is essentially useless or has a better alternative, people will soon stop buying it.

When developing advertising for TV and the Internet, take into account the full range of its types:

  1. informative advertising, introducing a potential buyer to a new piece;
  2. selective advertising, aimed at a particular segment of the jewellery market (young people, newlyweds and so on);
  3. comparative advertising, stressing the advantages of the firm’s jewellery over competitors’ goods;
  4. reminder advertising, used as a rule by well-known jewellery firms and consisting of the firm’s name (it reminds buyers to buy that particular firm’s jewellery);
  5. reinforcing advertising, whose aim is not to persuade the consumer to buy but to convince the buyer that the purchase was right.

An advertising message should contain a central thesis (the advertising argument) telling the potential buyer about the most important properties and distinctive features that make the jewellery useful. The following types of usefulness are distinguished:

  • rational usefulness, linked to meeting rational needs;
  • sensory usefulness, linked to the pleasure the consumer gets from owning the piece;
  • social-prestige usefulness, when owning the piece raises the owner’s prestige in their social circle;
  • self-affirmation usefulness, when owning the piece raises the buyer’s self-esteem.

For a potential buyer to perceive advertising correctly, three conditions must be met:

  1. the information must be presented to the audience correctly;
  2. the audience must remember the main idea of the message;
  3. the audience must respond with the right action.

The first condition matters because people are usually receptive to information that fits their established views and beliefs, so those views must be taken into account. The second is hard because consumers blend the text of the ad with their earlier ideas, often reading in meanings its creators did not intend. Finally, even remembered information will not necessarily push a person to act if it runs against their beliefs — hence the difficulty of the third condition.

This leads to the basic principles of developing advertising:

  1. What is perceived consciously usually stays longer in memory.
  2. What is said at the beginning and end of a message is remembered better than what is said in the middle.
  3. Varied or unusual material is remembered better.
  4. Information is remembered and received more easily if it does not contradict habitual notions, beliefs or opinions.

Running commercials during shows and news programmes gives good results.

Stylistically, any advertising message suits TV commercials.

There are several standard approaches to TV advertising:

  1. Slice of life: showing the product in a familiar setting.
  2. Lifestyle: emphasising how the product fits a particular way of life.
  3. Fantasy: creating an aura of the fantastic, unreal or illusory around the piece or its use.
  4. Mood or image: the product is meant to evoke a mood — the beauty of one’s surroundings, serenity, romantic dreams.
  5. Musical: characters singing a song about the product.
  6. A symbolic character personifying the product.
  7. Technical and professional expertise: showing the firm’s experience in making a particular product, often stressing its many years in business.
  8. Scientific evidence: data showing the product’s greater effectiveness or better quality compared with one or more other brands.
  9. Testimonial: a highly credible or likeable source speaks approvingly of the product — a celebrity or ordinary people saying how much they like it.

Developing advertising has two stages. At the first, you decide on the structure of the message and formulate the main points and how to present them. At the second, you develop the advertising material: write the text, choose words, illustrations and colours. Follow these principles:

  • the message must attract attention;
  • its structure must suit the chosen medium;
  • the text must be simple;
  • use only words and graphics familiar to potential buyers;
  • the design matters as much as the content;
  • stress the importance to the consumer of the piece’s hidden inner qualities over the outward qualities that can be checked and assessed.

Designing brochures about a jewellery company

Every firm that produces advertising materials for professionals tries to make them stand out and be useful so they do not end up in the bin.

Do not economise: photographs of pieces and interiors must be professional. The pieces must look their best, because customers will order your products from the catalogue. For shoots, use a model of the type relevant to your target audience.

In the diamond business, for example, the most common brochures are professional guidelines for grading rough and polished diamonds. Most often they contain tables relating a diamond’s weight to its dimensions (diameter, or length and width). Some more detailed brochures give characteristics of colour, clarity and cut quality. Professionals like such materials if they are made as cardboard or plastic dispensers or unusually shaped calendars that can stand on a desk.

Scientific gemmological centres publish books about stones as gifts for their clients. The “Coloured Stones Handbook” published by the Dubai laboratory for precious metals and stones looks good. It gives a popular account of the mineralogical characteristics of each stone (colour, hardness and lustre, for example) and its main deposits.

The same laboratory published a small brochure on gold alloys. Its explanation of the composition of “white gold” is very interesting.

If a laboratory specialises in serious scientific research, its brochures usually show modern equipment and research results. The Swiss Gemmological Institute, for example, includes photographs of microscopic inclusions in natural rubies, sapphires and emeralds. A separate page covers the differences between natural and cultured pearls. Specialists are also interested to learn that the laboratory has equipment for detecting treated diamonds, so one section of the brochure is devoted to spectroscopy.

For a firm with its own sources of jewellery raw materials, materials on the crystallography and geology of the deposit are convincing. A brochure about a demantoid deposit, for example, is very illustrative.

Wholesale jewellery companies explain in their materials how to display collections — offering promotion guidelines together with display equipment.

Jewellery companies selling diamond jewellery to end consumers have in recent years issued brochures explaining why one diamond costs more than another. Tiffany’s brochure and the “Love Diamonds” collection brochure deserve attention.

The Tahitian pearl and Philippine pearl associations have begun issuing guides to the quality and colour characteristics of sea pearls.

The trademark logo

The SUNLIGHT case

How the SUNLIGHT logo evolved

  1. 2007

    SUNLIGHT™ “Solnechny Svet”The Latin name with a Russian translation and the line “stylish jewellery with diamonds”: the logo explains who we are and what we sell. This is how it appears on the cover of “What a Jewellery Appraiser Should Know”.

  2. 2008

    The sun emblemAn emblem appears — a sun-flower — and the translation and tagline disappear. The name is now known well enough to speak for itself. Cover of Brilliant Life.

  3. Next

    SUNLIGHT BRILLIANTThe name sits inside a polygon echoing the outline of a cut stone; the word BRILLIANT bets on diamonds as the main product.

  4. Next

    WordmarkLetters only: white, widely spaced type on the brand red. A clean mark that reads well on a store sign and a shopping bag.

  5. Today

    The “S” iconA red rounded square with a double-looped “S” — essentially a mobile-app icon — next to the spaced-out SUNLIGHT wordmark. Today the main shop window is a phone screen, and the logo is made for it.

Who designed it. According to Sergey Gribnyakov, the SUNLIGHT typeface and mark changed about five times: one version was made by Artemy Lebedev’s studio, the next by another agency, the fourth by Denis Simachev, the fifth by the Unica Creative Engineering studio. The last change came with the shift of sales into the app (about 8 million downloads): the logo had to say at once that this is an online store. The downside of frequent changes is that for a while stores in the chain carry different versions of the brand identity (more in chapter 4).

What this case teaches. A logo moves from description to symbol: while a brand is unknown, it explains what it sells; once it is known, a name and a sign are enough. The form of the mark follows the main sales channel — from book cover and shop sign to app icon. Through every change the SUNLIGHT name and the red colour stayed, so customers recognise the brand at every stage.

For remembering a brand, the name matters more than the trademark symbol. Studies compared logos with names against the same logos without names. Subjects failed to recognise many logos once the names were removed. In other words, a name is far more effective than a symbol. A good, memorable name gives a product a good start; a bad name can become a big problem.

When developing a logo, consider the following:

  1. Shape. A logo should be evenly rectangular, as that is how the human eye perceives it best. A logo stretched too far vertically or horizontally is perceived worse. The biggest mistake is making a logo illegible, which often happens when companies make the symbol larger than the name. Legibility is the key factor in choosing a logo. The choice of colour also matters.
  2. Colour. “Warm” colours such as red, orange and yellow catch the eye and attract attention. They are high-energy and good for retail. Shades of blue are “cool” and “conservative”: they seem to recede from the viewer, muting and generalising the picture. Black and gold are considered symbols of prestige. Bright colours are usually perceived as simpler and more cheerful.

Logos that are an abbreviated name are often poorly received. They work only when they abbreviate the name of a well-known company.

Advertising today: what has changed

This chapter draws largely on magazine, TV and brochure advertising of the 1990s and 2000s. Back then this was right: the main customers read magazines and watched television. The chapter’s principles — AIDA, simplicity, repetition, a consistent style, the power of the name — still work today. But the channels, formats and rules have changed beyond recognition.

ThenNow
A magazine module and a TV commercialShort vertical video, in-app banners, mailings, creator content
One ad for everyoneDifferent messages for different audiences, testing of variants
Effect judged by eyeEvery placement is measured: reach, clicks, sales, cost per customer
A celebrity in the adCreators and customers themselves: reviews, photos, unboxings
A brochure as a giftExpert content: guides, articles, a gemmologist’s videos
Rules are just the advertising lawEvery online ad must be labelled, and some platforms are banned

Video first. Jewellery sells best in motion. The first seconds decide whether a clip is watched, so AIDA’s “attention” has become even stricter: sparkle, a hand, a face, a surprise — straight away.

Creators and customers. Recommendations from real people earn more trust than studio shoots. Not only famous creators work but also small authors with engaged audiences, as well as customers’ own photos and reviews. The recommender must genuinely fit the brand: the chapter’s rule to co-promote only with those who increase trust still applies.

Measurement. Today you can calculate what it cost to attract one customer and how much they brought over time. Without this, advertising is a gift to the platforms. Try calculating your channels in the Practicum.

Law and labelling. Online advertising in Russia must be labelled: the word “Advertisement”, the advertiser’s details and a unique erid identifier obtained through an advertising data operator. Since 1 September 2025, advertising has been banned on resources of organisations recognised as extremist or undesirable, including Instagram and Facebook (owned by Meta, which is designated extremist and banned in Russia), and on resources with restricted access. Platform rules keep changing, so check them before launching a campaign. A new rule specific to jewellers: from 1 September 2026 a lab-grown stone cannot be called a brilliant in advertising (see chapter 11).

AI in advertising. Neural networks help produce text and image variants quickly for testing. But an image of a piece must match the real item: if the customer receives something different from what they saw, trust is lost.

What still holds. Without an idea, no amount of money will save an ad. The product’s difference, the customer’s benefit, simplicity, repetition and a recognisable style are the foundation on which all the new channels work.

Summary and exercises

Key points

  • A name is remembered better than a symbol; a logo changes with the main sales channel while keeping the name and colour.
