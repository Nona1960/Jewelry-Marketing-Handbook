Chapter 4

Strategies of competitive warfare

Business is war. Retreat is a sign of weakness. In today’s turbulent world you must always be able to stay on your feet. In competition it is vital to fight to complete victory. You cannot settle for second place. And it is very important to ignore momentary criticism and aim for distant goals.

Competition gives energy, rewards winners and punishes losers. It is the fuel of the economy. You must always be able to fight back. Rupert Murdoch has said, in effect, that if someone beats you half to death and you don’t leave them with at least a couple of bruises, they will do the same to the next person in their way — and it would do a company no good to be seen as a nonentity that can be crushed and squeezed with impunity.

Sometimes it is very important to be able to sow fear both among your own staff and among competitors with a “signature” mix of empty threats and real displays of power. Do not speak ill of competitors. But if your customer faces a real or potential threat from them, be sure to warn the customer — politely, avoiding epithets and sticking to the facts.

Forget complexity and “long” market strategies. Concentrate on the four pillars of marketing: price, promotion, place of distribution and the product itself.

The road to meeting market demands requires a company to:

  • supply the market with products of the highest quality;
  • remember that the customer’s interests come first;
  • turn customer satisfaction into loyalty;
  • motivate employees and try to keep them;
  • anticipate competitors’ moves;
  • treat marketing spending not as a cost but as an investment, and strengthen trademarks, which are also company assets.

Several widely recognised strategies help win the competitive war in the jewellery market:

1. Know what your competitor offers

Buy your main competitors’ products or use their services to see what they do differently from you, and you will learn a great deal about your customers’ expectations.

Positive opinions of a firm appear when reality exceeds expectations, and negative ones when expectations are not met. Establish what additional benefits you can give customers compared with what they expect — this will encourage positive reviews. If you let any parameter fall below expectations, you will almost certainly get negative reviews.

It is also very important to know similar promotional products released by your competitors. If you plan to make something similar to what is already on the market, customers may be more receptive. Many management theories advise against dealing with something completely new and risky. Buyers will accept something substantially original, but not entirely unfamiliar.

A few tips for winning the competitive battle:

  • You will be more successful proposing a new idea that involves only minor changes than offering a radically new product.
  • You can increase the value of your idea by pointing to secondary, long-term, hidden and indirect benefits or side advantages. But put the emphasis on short-term or immediate benefits; secondary benefits are merely good support for them.
  • Make sure your ideas do not contradict the main trends of the fashion industry.

2. Study the jewellery market

To analyse the market, prepare a report answering these questions:

  1. What share of the market do competing firms control? How quickly are they entering the market?
  2. How are competing firms doing in terms of revenue and introducing new jewellery designs?
  3. What is the subject of the fiercest competition in this market segment (price, quality, company image)?
  4. How much attention and money do competitors devote to advertising their jewellery? Which advertising agencies do they use?
  5. What are competitors’ products like (main characteristics, quality level, design, etc.)?
  6. What are competitors’ price levels and pricing policies?
  7. What is the image of the competing firms?

Qualities to develop in order to succeed

On finding your niche in the jewellery business

To continue this theme: I remember we were taken on study trips — to Germany (Idar-Oberstein), to the exchanges in Amsterdam and Antwerp. They tried to give us some knowledge of diamond certification, and the impressions of diamonds stayed with me for life. But it all passed in the background; I remember practically nothing of those lectures and master classes. I only remember that the tour of the world’s diamond centres was interesting overall.

Perhaps not the most fitting example, but let me quote Ostap Bender: if there are printed banknotes, someone must have a great many of them. During or after those trips I had ideas which I shared with my teacher, Nona Dronova: if there are jewellers in Russia at all and the country has a jewellery industry, then we too have first-class jewellers, leaders by every world criterion. The best professionals have a worthy assortment and priceless, rich experience, and it is interesting to get to know these people, whether in Russia, China or any other country.

The head of a jewellery company must be able to formulate its concept so that employees believe in it and come to regard it as their own. A concept has little value until it is understood. An effective concept should rest on analogies, metaphors and vivid explanations, as well as on emotions, trust and hope.

A jewellery company can prosper for many years if it has a clear plan of action and has defined how to carry it out. Planning should include the following steps:

  • Set a clear goal. First decide on the direction of your business.
  • Ask yourself what has already been achieved. Analyse your strengths and weaknesses; this will let you move confidently towards the goal.
  • Identify the most important tasks. The company should single out the three main lines of business that bring the most profit.
  • Define short-term tasks. The near horizon is 12 months.
  • Define a strategy for each task. Strategy differs from tactics: tactics are the sequence of actions in carrying out a particular strategy.

Strategy is a more general, broader plan of the actions needed to achieve three main goals:

  1. Stay ahead in your game. Often the difference between first and second place depends not on how good you are but on whether you can stay on top.
  2. Know your enemies. The market today is fiercely competitive. If you have an ace up your sleeve, don’t show it until it is time to act.
  3. Shoot at once, without aiming. You cannot allow yourself to be second. Aiming too long, you may miss the right moment and be dead before you fire. Better to arrive early and take a good position.
  4. There is sad experience of successful firms trying to reuse the ideas that brought them success. But in a constantly changing world ideas often become obsolete overnight. What worked in the past will not necessarily work in the future. To keep up, you must constantly generate new ideas.

Realising your personality is directly linked to persistence, self-knowledge, readiness to take risks, devotion to your work and the ability to challenge circumstances.

The situation in the jewellery business reflects the mood of the business community as a whole. Some are rising, some declining; some are planning a rebrand, others a deal with strategically important partners. We asked Sergey Gribnyakov, founder and owner of the SUNLIGHT jewellery chain, what interesting things have been happening on the Russian and international jewellery and gemstone markets.

– Sergey Anatolyevich, what is competition, in your view?

– You want a textbook definition? Many people confuse competition and competitiveness; they think they are the same thing…

– Competitiveness is when a person has qualities that help them win against rivals.

– Yes, including the ability to change quickly, I suppose. So many different challenges! If you are proactive, open to the new, curious, you will cope with them.

– Competition is a struggle. Have you experienced it in your life and business? Did you worry that a competitor was better than you?

– No, never, because the market in Russia… how to put it? On the contrary, I feel there are so many opportunities around, an endless number, and people lack the sense to see these simple things. The opportunities lie right on the surface, they are so easy to realise and there are so many of them — just take them and make them happen! So I have never been jealous of anyone in business. On the contrary, it seemed that it was all small stuff and that everyone preferred to mark time.

– That’s exactly how it is. And internationally recognised jewellery firms — Tiffany, Cartier and others — do you feel them as competitors? Or as teachers?

– That is probably a matter of national mindset. They are ahead in education and experience… There, dozens of generations have worked in the jewellery market for hundreds of years — and always more successfully than other countries. That is geo-competition. So of course you look at the leading foreign brands and learn. There are questions and tasks they solve far more professionally than we do in Russia. We just have to learn.

– When you started your business, your first professional activity was trading gold chains. There were firms competing with you: the Bronnitsy Jewellery Plant, Adamas… Now you have overtaken them. What can you tell us about your first dealings with these companies? They were the biggest players in the domestic jewellery market. Adamas, for example, later went bankrupt… Why?

– All firms go bankrupt sometime. When Adamas was the largest Russian jewellery company, we earned ten per cent of its turnover selling its own products. Every company reaches a peak at some point and then declines. Businesses go through different stages, so what is there to say?

– As a young businessman then, what impressed you about a company as big as Adamas?

– Everything impressed me: the structure, how the managers worked… When Adamas was already in its prime I was so inexperienced in commerce that every business process interested me: the negotiations themselves, how many staff there were, what the employees did and how… Especially since in that post-Soviet time all these business nuances were in short supply. There was no proper, fundamental business education. Everything had to be invented from scratch. So the companies that succeeded were, of course, authorities. I watched them and learned.

– The first time you came to Adamas to negotiate the purchase of chains — how were you received? How did they talk to you as a young entrepreneur?

– I don’t even remember what the first negotiations were like — there were no negotiations, we were so much smaller than Adamas. There was a queue there, I suppose, of people like me.

– You once told me in an interview about the Bronnitsy plant and mentioned that you didn’t like their packaging.

– Why? On the contrary, I liked it. It was awkward to transport, but it impressed me: rigid cardboard with a stamp that you couldn’t tear. Somebody designed it — probably some Soviet institute. And those delicate chains, which tend to suffer in transit, really were as if in armour. And it looked, you know, the way precious metals should…

– Respectful, yes?

– Respectful. You could see that in Soviet times the jewellery industry was so state-controlled and so free of competition that producers could afford touches like that austere packaging. There was no healthy competition, hence such examples… There were interesting things in the past that you simply cannot see now. Nobody would do that today.

– Back then it was chains, now it is diamonds. You are a major supplier and have your own brand, SERGEY GRIBNYAKOV “Diamonds of Yakutia”. Is it interesting to work with diamonds? What have you discovered, what have you understood?

– A different average receipt, I suppose. A higher receipt, a different customer… You have to adapt. It doesn’t happen quickly; you still have to learn to work with these customers. So of course I would like growth to be faster. But some lag is inevitable: any new business requires reformatting, training, fine-tuning. For now I believe consumers underrate our brand and that it should sell much better.

– When you talk with the biggest players in the diamond market, do you notice anything interesting that unites them? What is happening in this segment of the world jewellery industry today?

– Diamond suppliers today are global transnational corporations. India leads. As I understand it, there are several companies in that country with large shares of the diamond markets and of world diamond trade overall. They both buy and cut rough diamonds, and in essence they are now semi-financial organisations. They offer not so much diamonds as a mix of gemstones and banking products.

– What does that mean?

– The terms, the way they work, the contracts — the owners of these companies think on such a scale! Their interests are different. It is not about bringing ten stones from some factory or delivering ten stones there. Their business is built on plans for market share across a whole country. That is how transnational corporations work. Their plan is to take not three per cent of the market but, say, seventy. And they may do that in America, in Europe and in Africa. These corporations open factories all over the world.

– Does this business experience help you? Perhaps marketing advice, how to promote products, or further professional education? Or the art of negotiation: one word said, and it decides the fate of a deal?

– This field is partly new to me. If you work in it for five or ten years, your business horizons of course widen noticeably. You watch how foreign entrepreneurs work, how transnational corporations work. Everything is different there.

– Tell us how it is different.

– The competences of transnational-corporation staff responsible for new territories today include a foreign language, sometimes more than one. And it seems to me that staff motivation, somewhere deep down, at a genetic level, is an order of magnitude higher. A quarter of a billion people in India currently live below the poverty line — that strongly motivates people to secure better living conditions for themselves. There is also a particular business culture there, in which far higher demands are placed on a person than in Russia.

I visit my stores sometimes — as often as I can. And in half of the cases when I visited a SUNLIGHT store, those foreign managers were there. I am amazed how they find time to look at things and compare… In fact, my entire business is studied by them from start to finish. Here, we have very few specialists with that level of immersion and motivation to deliver results for the company.

– What motivates them? The company’s results, their own career?

– Here in Russia financial motivation matters more to people. There it is probably a secondary question. In India caste differences still exist, and family traditions add to them. If a person is from another caste and works not in the family business, not for relatives, the employer’s demands on them are very high. For the salary paid, they expect full immersion in the job and total dedication. And people give their all and value the fact that they work in that position.

– Did these managers tell you where they studied and what education they had?

– I asked the bosses themselves how they handle their own children. In our last conversation I gathered that they try not to bring their offspring into the company straight away but send them to other enterprises, other businesses, preferably larger than their own. Then, after a while, when the heirs have worked in other companies, the parents take them into the family firm. That is their approach: to raise a good entrepreneur and manager indirectly, so that the son or daughter, with some professional experience, looks at the older generation’s business plans more objectively, from the vantage point of the outside world.

– A commendable tradition!.. We are now going through documents, archive tags, the first SUNLIGHT catalogues. Part of the company’s history is the change of logo. How many times did it change, and why?

– I think sometimes it didn’t need to be done. At times the old logo was better than the new one. But it depends on many factors: the external environment, consultants, an advertising or design agency… It pays an agency to propose changes and get paid for them, so its creatives think about what to change. And in the end they may convince the client that they are right about something, that something was superfluous.

– Tell us at least a little about the history of the SUNLIGHT logo.

– We changed the typeface perhaps five times. First there were thick letters close together, then thinner ones, then a slightly different typeface… Once Artemy Lebedev did it, the next time another company. The fourth time Simachev changed the typeface. The fifth time it was the Unica Creative Engineering design studio — there are talented people there too; we asked them to do it. Retail simply began to change, and a logo was needed that would work in app mode. The app appeared on almost everyone’s phone — there were about eight million downloads. So on the phone the logo took on a different function. It had to explain that here, in the app, there is no longer an ordinary store but an online store. And all that had to be done in a single picture, and different companies solved some of these tasks differently. That is why the SUNLIGHT corporate identity went through changes.

It is, of course, an expensive pleasure when half your stores have one typeface and logo and the other half a slightly different design, with another typeface and logo. It undoubtedly undermines the overall integrity of brand perception. But I believe it is better to change than to reject innovation altogether. Many world-famous companies have changed their typefaces and logos: Apple, Google, VKontakte and others. Following trends, everyone changes little by little.

– Let’s recall the first SUNLIGHT promotions, which were very successful. It is very interesting! And readers will remember which chains, rings and earrings they bought and when… Remember the product catalogue you made? On what scale did you distribute it? The internet was not so developed then, and the catalogue went down very well.

– I don’t remember — maybe a million, maybe half a million. Print runs by the wagonload, anyway.

– And you handed them out? Tell us where.

– We handed them to customers in stores and sent them by post. We did everything with them!

– Do you think it was a successful campaign at the time?

– Yes, we calculated it — I no longer remember the figures. The catalogue was successful then: we had a new brand, a new assortment, many new designs. We printed it all, made it beautiful. When print runs are large, the result can be more impressive than with small coverage. Of course, at that time the company could grow by a hundred per cent a year. That is hard to repeat now, but those were the results then.

– Remember the gifts SUNLIGHT used to give away?

– We still give them away.

– Which gifts were most successful?

– Gifts in general were and remain the most successful promotion. The effect depends not so much on the surprise itself as on the promotion channel: which audience of buyers received the gift. We were working with new customer databases. You can take one from Auchan, or from VkusVill or another retail chain. You can take a selective one from a bank — only wealthy customers. So these campaigns performed quite differently, depending not on the gift but on the audience.

– And which audience performed best in those days — the bank’s or Auchan’s?

– You have to count the total: how much was spent and what the return was in the end. A customer can be expensive but bring in a lot, while a large audience obtained for free may give a less efficient return. But the best performer — if memory serves, let me try to recall — was the database of women’s birthdays.

– Excellent, super! And finally: SUNLIGHT rarely takes part in the trade fairs held at VDNKh in Moscow or in St Petersburg… Why this policy? Others build huge stands to attract attention, while you practically never take part.

– I believe B2B companies should take part in trade fairs, but there is no point for B2C companies working with the end customer. Our customer is in a shopping centre in a big city; our customer today is somewhere on the internet. A trade fair is a drop in the ocean that plays no role for us at all.

Of course we do take part in fairs together with jewellery manufacturers. We meet them there; our buyers and developers of new collections are all at the fairs. Our specialists attend industry events not only in Russia but also in Italy, Dubai, Thailand, Hong Kong and China. That is their job: they are there looking for new trends, directions and niches to show our customers. Perhaps somewhere there is the same product as ours, but better and cheaper, which means we can offer our customers more competitive prices. But what is the point of offering our goods at a fair where most visitors are professionals themselves, representatives of B2B trade? No point at all.

What prospects do you see for SUNLIGHT?

Our companies are now merging into one, and tomorrow the whole turnover will be visible. In short, we will “wrap” ordinary life and ordinary retail into an IT business, and all the steps of this transition will gradually be revealed. The IT business will account for 60–80%: databases, database management, connecting the website to the database — there are many stages, but that is a question for the managers who will join us; naturally they care about the technologies they will be working with.

How is jewellery retail changing?

Here I do not put myself above the professionals. That is the idea for now — I deal with this question as much as I can, because IT fascinates me. If the development of a classic business is a book, these are its final chapters: you conclude that you already have an IT company. The proof is that almost your entire advertising budget is spent on attracting customers online, and that the website and the retail stores have practically drawn level in sales. Stores bring in half at best, and soon they will start to fall behind. So this is now a “book” for IT managers and programmers. Eighty per cent of commercial success today comes not from artistic personalities but from digital technologies, which arose and, improving, complemented and replaced one another, delivered unconventional and effective business solutions and raised the value of companies. It is not always easy to get my thoughts, ideas and proposals in this field across to the team, but I am working on it.

Summary and exercises

Key points

  • Competition in jewellery retail is a fight for the customer’s attention and trust, not only on price.
  • Success starts with your own niche: SUNLIGHT arrived at the “jewellery hypermarket” format with affordable prices and a huge choice.
  • A brand identity has to change as the sales channel changes — from shop sign to app icon; the price is an inconsistent chain during the transition.
  • Jewellery retail is moving online and into apps; whoever changes before the competitors wins.
