Trends 2026: what is changing jewellery marketing right now

The jewellery market is changing faster than ever. The stone itself is changing: lab-grown diamonds have arrived, and new rules have been adopted around them. The customer journey is changing: video and AI assistants are replacing the shop window and the search engine. The meaning of trust is changing too. This chapter covers what is happening in 2026 and how marketing should respond. The figures come from public sources, listed at the end.

Lab-grown stones and the new rules

The main topic of the year is lab-grown diamonds, which are rapidly gaining share. At the 585 Zolotoy chain, the share of lab-grown pieces among diamond jewellery rose from 2.4% to 6.9% in the first quarter of 2026. At SOKOLOV, lab-grown pieces account for about 25% of sales of jewellery with precious stones. The reason is price: according to SOKOLOV, a ring with a 1-carat natural diamond costs from 600,000 roubles, and one with a lab-grown stone about 50,000.

The state responded with new rules. Russian Government Decree No. 657 of 30 May 2026 took effect on 1 September 2026. Under it:

  • only a natural stone may be called a “diamond” (brilliant); a lab-grown one is called a “faceted synthetic diamond”;
  • the weight of a synthetic stone is given in grams, not carats, and it is not described by colour and clarity grades;
  • the words “natural”, “genuine”, “eco-friendly” and “real” cannot be applied to synthetics;
  • according to the Ministry of Finance, pieces put on sale before 1 September are sold under the old rules.

The market split. The Russian Jewellers’ Guild and 585 Zolotoy opposed some of the rules, while Rostzoloto and Sim-Sim supported them. At the same time, on 10 June 2026 ALROSA adopted its first long-term programme for promoting natural diamonds through 2030.

What this means for marketing. First, check every tag, product card, ad and collection name: calling a lab-grown stone a brilliant is now a violation. Second, choose a position: a natural stone sells through rarity, story, heritage and investment, a lab-grown one through affordability and “more stone for the same money”. Trying to say both in one message breeds distrust. Third, right now customers need an expert who honestly explains the difference. A gemmological laboratory (see Science) becomes a marketing advantage.

Trust is the main product

The more similar-looking stones there are on the market, the more certainty is worth. A survey of more than 3,000 Russians by the Smart Diamonds brand (585 Zolotoy) found that only 35% of those who wear diamonds know where their stones come from. A certificate of authenticity is trusted by 68% of respondents, more than an independent expert assessment (44%). People prefer to buy diamonds in physical stores (76%) and on official brand websites (28%), seeing these as the most reliable channels.

Lesson. Transparency is not a duty but a sales tool. A certificate, a clear tag, traceability through GIIS DMDK and the option of a lab check should all be shown to the customer, not hidden in small print.

Demand and affordability

In the first quarter of 2026, unit sales of jewellery fell by 5–10% year on year, according to various analysts. Buyers are becoming more careful: young couples increasingly choose moissanite rings instead of diamonds. At the same time demand is polarising. Grant analysts note that in 2026 two price segments lead at once: 40,000–60,000 and 200,000–400,000 roubles.

Lesson. The middle is under pressure. The winners clearly serve either affordability (wide choice, instalments, promotions) or value (natural stones, brand, service). A brand portfolio like SUNLIGHT’s covers both ends.

AI is changing the customer journey

Customers increasingly start not with a search engine but with a question to an AI assistant: “which engagement ring should I choose?”, “how is a lab-grown diamond different from a natural one?”. The assistant answers by citing sources it trusts. International agencies advise jewellery brands to prepare content for this: detailed gemstone guides, honest comparisons of natural and lab-grown diamonds, and articles signed by an expert author.

AI also works inside the store: personal recommendations, gift selection, answers about sizing and care, generated descriptions and images. Market reviews note that AI sales agents and live video consultations are changing how expensive pieces sell online.

Lesson. Write expert content for people, and machines will find it. A textbook, an encyclopaedia, a gemstone guide signed by a gemmologist are the best way into AI answers. The WIKI SUNLIGHT encyclopaedia is an example of this approach (see Media).

Virtual try-on

Augmented reality lets customers “try on” a ring or earrings through a phone camera. Global houses such as Chanel, Bulgari and Louis Vuitton already use such tools. It is important to be realistic about their role: industry analysts say virtual try-on mainly removes uncertainty early in the choice rather than closing an expensive sale.

Lesson. Virtual try-on is the first step, and trying on in store is the last. The chain “try in the app → reserve → try in person” links online and offline.

The secondary market and trade-in

Resale and pre-owned jewellery are going mainstream. Analysts note that this market rewards trust rather than stock volume: honest grading, authentication and a guarantee. For Russian chains this means pawnshops, buy-back and trading old gold for new.

Lesson. Trade-in and buy-back are not a side business but a way to bring the customer back to the store and lower the threshold for the next purchase.

Video and live contact

Discovery of new brands is moving to short-form video, and expensive purchases are increasingly accompanied by live video consultations. SUNLIGHT Play and customer video chats (see the previous chapter) are a Russian example of the same movement.

Lesson. Jewellery sells best in motion: sparkle, play of light, a hand, a face. Every key piece needs a video.

A checklist for modern jewellery marketing

  1. Check that tags, website and ads comply with the synthetic-stone rules in force from 1 September 2026.
  2. Choose and clearly state a position: natural, lab-grown, or both under different marks.
  3. Show proof of trust: certificate, hallmark, GIIS DMDK, laboratory expertise.
  4. Create expert content signed by a specialist: guides, comparisons, answers to common questions.
  5. Prepare videos of key pieces and try live video consultations.
  6. Link the app and the store: reservation, try-on, fast delivery.
  7. Develop trade-in and buy-back as an entry point for the next purchase.
  8. Measure every channel: what it costs to attract a customer and how much they bring over time (see Practicum).

Summary and exercises

Key points

  • From 1 September 2026, a lab-grown stone in Russia cannot be called a brilliant: it is a “faceted synthetic diamond”, weighed in grams.
  • Trust has become the main product: certificates, hallmarks, traceability and expertise sell better than words.
  • Demand is polarising: either affordability or value wins; the middle is under pressure.
  • The customer journey runs through AI assistants, video and in-app try-on; expert content with a named author gets into AI answers.
