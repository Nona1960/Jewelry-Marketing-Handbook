Value factors of jewellery products

In jewellery manufacturing, the notion of value combines several factors: price, product quality, the speed and quality of service to wholesale customers, and new collections.

Price. Customers want to pay as little as possible for a product or service while demanding consistent quality.

Quality. In some categories of jewellery, special value is created by quality, and price does not matter. People are willing to pay for an exclusive product or service. Moreover, if the price is not high enough to confirm the quality, customers begin to wonder whether they are really buying something exclusive.

Speed of service. Companies able to fulfil customer orders quickly must do so. People do not like waiting long for their orders.

Quality of service. Customers must be sure they can contact the company at any time and receive guaranteed service, including repairs.

New collections. Constant renewal of the assortment is very important in the jewellery market. People want everything newest and most up to date. In the new millennium the answer for companies is “survival of the fastest”: companies must not only change and reinvent themselves, they must do it faster and faster. The leader must stay ahead of the industry, anticipating and outpacing its changes. They must be able to analyse the situation and make the right decisions quickly today, so that tomorrow the company is in the right place with the right resources, even if the picture of the future is not yet clear. One successful company, for example, set itself the corporate goal of introducing a new product category every year and earning a third of its annual income from new products.

In planning jewellery production one must rely on the principle of “understanding the consumer”, which takes into account consumer needs and the dynamics of the jewellery market. Under this principle, all of a jewellery company’s activity is built on knowledge of consumer demand and how it will change. It makes production a function of market demand and requires jewellery to be produced in the assortment and volume the consumer needs.

In defining a marketing strategy, one should proceed from the following marketing concepts:

  1. The production concept: jewellery will sell if it is widely available and affordable. Management that chooses this concept must give strategic priority to improving production (lowering production costs) and making distribution channels more efficient.
  2. The product concept: jewellery of the highest quality and best characteristics will sell. Management that chooses this concept must focus its efforts on improving the collection of designs.
  3. The selling concept: jewellery will sell if the company makes significant efforts in sales and sales promotion.
  4. The marketing (consumer) concept: jewellery will sell if the company correctly identifies the needs and wants of target markets and satisfies them more effectively and efficiently than competitors.

In developing its marketing strategy, a jewellery company may adopt one or several of these concepts, and this will determine the size of its marketing budget.

Depending on the state of demand in the jewellery market, the following types of marketing are distinguished:

  1. Conversional marketing — for negative demand, when most buyers reject certain jewellery designs. The task is to develop a marketing plan that creates demand for that type of jewellery.
  2. Stimulational marketing — for jewellery with no demand because consumers are completely indifferent to it. The task is to find the reasons for the indifference and ways to overcome it.
  3. Developmental marketing — for emerging demand for new collections still being designed. It is used when there is latent demand (a need for a piece of jewellery that does not yet exist on the market as a product). Its aim is to turn latent demand into real demand.
  4. Remarketing. At a certain stage of its life cycle, demand for any kind of jewellery falls. The aim of remarketing is to revive demand and use new marketing opportunities.
  5. Synchromarketing — used under fluctuating demand to stabilise sales and minimise fluctuations.
  6. Maintenance marketing — used when the level and structure of demand fully match the level and structure of supply. Here a well-considered pricing policy, targeted advertising and properly directed sales promotion are needed.

A jewellery company’s set of marketing activities usually includes:

  • studying jewellery consumers and their behaviour in the market;
  • evaluating the jewellery produced and the prospects for improving it;
  • analysing the forms and channels of distribution used;
  • evaluating the firm’s pricing methods;
  • examining activities to promote jewellery on the market;
  • studying competitors;
  • choosing the most favourable market segment.

The basis of a jewellery company’s successful market policy is winning a real market share — the part of the jewellery market the firm uses to sell its pieces. Market share can change over time depending on how actively the company uses marketing factors (market research, advertising, pricing, sales promotion).

A jewellery company’s success depends heavily on how well it carries out market segmentation — dividing all consumers into groups that differ by social position, income, profession, needs and preferences, and age. How soundly the segment is chosen largely determines the firm’s success in competition.

The selected segments must meet several requirements:

  1. Each segment must be clearly defined, i.e. the consumer characteristics that place people in it must be identified.
  2. The segments must give the firm the necessary return: they must be large enough to cover the extra costs of adapting products to that segment and to bring a profit.
  3. The segments must be accessible to effective sales methods: there must be distribution channels and affordable advertising media.
  4. The segments must match the firm’s experience with that type of buyer.
  5. The selected segments must be as well protected from competition as possible.

Today, regional segmentation (population size, density, etc.) and demographic segmentation (age, gender, education, etc.) can be used to divide jewellery consumers.

Especially important at the current stage of market development is segmentation based on consumer typology, taking behavioural and psychological traits into account (occasions for purchase, intensity of consumption, ambition, etc.).

One of the most important elements of the marketing plan is pricing. The price of a piece of jewellery is linked to such marketing factors as its characteristics and image.

Jewellery prices can change over a product’s life cycle: from high prices to attract demanding, fashion- and prestige-oriented buyers, to low prices for the mass market.

The level of customer service depends on price. Low prices usually go with minimal service.

The price of a piece must be directly linked to its quality and should reflect the image of the jewellery firm.

The jewellery business has the notion of prestige pricing. A certain category of buyers will not buy jewellery at prices they consider too low. Such buyers set themselves lower price limits and buy nothing cheaper, believing that at a low price the quality will not meet their standards. At the same time consumers set upper limits they consider acceptable for particular items.

When analysing a jewellery company’s pricing methods, consider:

  • production cost;
  • competitors’ prices for similar jewellery;
  • the unique merits of the piece;
  • prices determined by demand for this type of jewellery.

Cost analysis usually gives the minimum possible price of a piece. Analysis of competitors’ prices gives the average price level for a given type of jewellery. The highest price is set for a piece with unique merits.

Demand-driven prices in the jewellery market can range from the minimum to the maximum.

To succeed, a jewellery company needs a purposeful pricing policy: setting prices for its pieces, and changing them with the market situation, so as to win a certain market share and make a profit.

The following situations must be taken into account:

  1. Entering a new market. To arouse buyers’ interest in a collection and gradually establish yourself in a new market, it makes sense to set prices lower than competitors’. Then, as you win the market and build a loyal clientele, prices are gradually raised to the level of other suppliers.
  2. Launching a new collection. Suppliers of particularly fashionable jewellery can afford especially high prices. But this policy must be limited in time, since high prices encourage competitors to make similar items. So at a certain point it is important to start lowering prices in order to win new segments.
  3. Defending your position. Every firm competing in the jewellery market tries to keep its existing share. Today non-price factors are considered dominant in competition — it is especially important to improve the consumer properties of products while keeping selling prices unchanged.
  4. Moving through market segments one by one. Original pieces are first offered in segments where buyers are ready to pay a high price, and then sold in segments where lower-quality items (synthetic stones, etc.) can be sold at a lower price.

An important element of the marketing plan is the scheme for distributing jewellery through sales channels. A sales channel is the route by which jewellery moves from producer to consumer.

A successful sales policy requires the following work:

  1. Research — collecting and analysing the information needed to plan and organise sales.
  2. Sales promotion — creating and spreading information about the piece that encourages potential buyers to purchase it.
  3. Making contacts — establishing and maintaining relations with potential buyers.
  4. Negotiation — agreeing prices and terms of sale.
  5. Financing — finding and using funds to cover the costs of running sales channels.
  6. Risk-taking — accepting responsibility for the functioning of sales channels.

A jewellery sales channel can be described by the number of its links or levels. A channel level is any intermediary that does some work to bring the piece closer to the end consumer.

In a one-level channel the intermediary is usually a retailer; in a two-level channel, a wholesaler and a retailer. In a three-level channel a small wholesaler stands between them, buying jewellery from large wholesalers and reselling it in small lots to retailers who are usually outside the large wholesalers’ attention.

For the head of a jewellery company, the effective solution is not to favour any single channel but to choose the best combination of several channels.

Approaches to building an assortment

When choosing an assortment, bear in mind that all jewellery can be roughly divided by quality into four groups: top, competitive, reduced and low.

Top-quality products surpass competitors’ similar goods. In many cases they are fundamentally new, often handmade. The maker of such jewellery can significantly increase profit both through higher prices and through a larger market share.

Competitive products mostly match a high level of quality but may also be of average quality among similar goods in the market. In the latter case competitiveness comes from more effective advertising and sales promotion and depends mainly on pricing, advertising and the choice of sales channels.

Products of reduced quality have somewhat worse consumer properties overall than most competitors’ products. To keep its market position stable, the producer may resort to a strategy of lowering prices on them.

Low-quality products are usually uncompetitive and either find no buyers at all or sell only at very low prices. The producer must raise their quality or develop new jewellery products.

In building an assortment, a firm can benefit greatly from marketing experience in studying product life cycles.

The life cycle of a piece of jewellery is the period during which it is developed and sold. The product life-cycle concept is used in creating and selling products and in developing marketing strategy from the moment a piece enters the market until it is withdrawn. The period a piece can stay on the market is limited, and the dynamics of its life determine the possible sales volume in each period while there is demand for it.

The following types of jewellery life cycles are usually distinguished:

  • traditional (gradual rise and fall of demand);
  • boom (rapid growth of demand that stays high for quite a long time);
  • fad (rapid rise and fall of demand);
  • seasonal (demand rising rhythmically with the seasons).

Despite the variety, the main stages of the life cycle can be identified: development, introduction, growth, maturity and decline. The move from one stage to another is marked by significant changes in the growth of sales and profit.

At the development stage, the piece’s basic parameters are worked out in detail: metal fineness, type of stones, artistic finishing, manufacturing technology, design, packaging and so on. Conclusions are drawn about how the buyer wants to see the piece today and tomorrow.

At the development stage it is useful to distinguish three levels:

  1. The piece as conceived: the core benefit the consumer gets from buying it.
  2. The piece as actually made: its properties, appearance, quality of stones, packaging.
  3. The augmented piece: sales arrangements and credit.

Introduction is the stage at which a new piece enters the market (first through test sales). The company must decide when, where, to whom and how to offer it. At this stage the product is a novelty. For consumers to recognise and accept it commercially, and for it to show its merits and distinctive features against competitors, considerable time and large spending on advertising and sales promotion are needed.

Sales volume may be small at first and grow very slowly, because buyers are inert towards anything new. In this period production capacity is underused, since jewellery is made in small and medium batches; profitability is low despite high prices, because production costs are high; competition is weak, since few firms are yet competing.

At this stage the firm should focus its marketing efforts on advertising, organising distribution channels and sales promotion, targeting mainly high-income consumers, since prices are usually high in this period.

Growth is the stage at which buyers accept the product and demand rises rapidly. Sales expand and variations of the piece appear. The number and volume of purchases grow, and so do the reputation and popularity of the jewellery firm as buyers share their opinions.

At this stage production capacity may fall short as production shifts towards mass output. Costs fall; prices stay fairly high, though somewhat lower than at introduction. The firm makes a high profit. Many jewellery companies enter the competition by making the same kind of jewellery, so the number of competitors can become considerable (pieces with coloured gemstones, pieces with a matt finish).

To extend the growth period, the firm can:

  1. improve the quality of the new product, giving it additional consumer properties;
  2. penetrate new segments of the existing market;
  3. redirect part of the advertising to strengthen motives for purchase;
  4. use new sales channels;
  5. lower the price in good time to attract additional buyers in the existing market;
  6. enter new markets.

Maturity (stabilisation) is the stage at which demand for the type of piece gradually becomes saturated. Demand growth slows and sales stabilise. Higher advertising spending, better quality and lower prices become necessary to sustain sales. Profitability can be kept at the previous level only by reducing production costs. Some excess capacity may appear. The pieces are produced in large batches. The market is saturated; the price structure and the distribution of market shares among competitors become more stable. At the end of this stage a decision must be made about the design — whether it will be improved or replaced by a new model.

Decline is the stage of falling demand and a sharp drop in sales and therefore profit. As production is cut, there is significant excess capacity. Firms begin to leave the competition, and the number of competitors falls sharply. Prices fall, although at the very end they may rise somewhat if demand exceeds supply because production of this kind of jewellery has been cut.

At the end of this stage the piece completes its life cycle and is pushed out of the market by the next generation of promising designs, since keeping a declining piece in the range can harm the company’s performance and reputation.

Thus a jewellery firm’s product policy consists of three elements: making timely changes to a piece; creating and launching new designs (platinum, avant-garde pieces); and discontinuing designs for which there is no demand.

The main drivers of product modification are consumer demands, national customs, the socio-cultural level of the population and the tax system.

Common practice in jewellery retail is this: an original piece that has proved popular should be followed by two similar models — one cheaper (using synthetic stones) and one more expensive and of better quality. This widens the market and therefore sales and profit.

A new piece of jewellery is a product that satisfies an entirely new need or satisfies a known need at a new level. Jewellery firms with a stable market position switch to completely new designs roughly every two years.

Launching new designs involves test sales: selling an original design in one or several chosen markets to observe how demand changes.

During test sales, information about buyers’ reactions to the design (appearance, advertising, price, etc.) is collected and analysed. As a result, changes may be made both to the product and to the firm’s marketing strategy for it. But already at the planning stage a schedule is set for phasing out the production and sale of the design, including the steps for reorienting the firm to new designs and restructuring the sales network.

When choosing a new type of jewellery, it is important to secure lasting competitiveness. Three types of competitive advantage should be distinguished: lower costs, differentiation and focus.

Lower costs means the firm’s ability to design, produce and sell a product at lower cost than its competitors. To gain this advantage, the entrepreneur must be able to organise the whole cycle — from design to sale to the consumer — at lower cost and in less time.

Differentiation is the ability to meet buyers’ special needs and receive a premium price for it, i.e. a price higher on average than competitors’. To stand out, the firm must offer jewellery that differs noticeably either in higher quality within a standard design (for example, gemstones of high quality and colour grades) or in a non-standard approach to design (an unusual combination of coloured gemstones or an original construction).

Focus means directing the firm’s efforts at a particular group of consumers (children’s jewellery, jewellery for teenagers, etc.) or at a limited geographical market (folk-style pieces).

At any moment an entrepreneur can build a competitiveness strategy on only one type of advantage: lower costs, attracting buyers with relatively cheaper goods; differentiation, appealing to the most demanding customers who want high quality above all; or focus, attracting a particular group of buyers.

The choice depends on the jewellery company’s capabilities. If it has outdated equipment and less qualified staff, but also low wages and production costs, it can adopt a strategy of making inexpensive, modest-quality jewellery for a less affluent (but usually numerous) group of buyers. Although the profit per piece will be small, the scale of production can be very large.

Another option: if high-quality gemstones are used and the staff includes highly qualified designers and model makers, it is worth seeking competitiveness through exclusive pieces or pieces of such high quality that, in buyers’ eyes, they justify their high price and gradually earn the company a reputation as a top-class maker.

Strategies for choosing competitive advantages

All competitive advantages in the jewellery business fall into two groups: lower-order and higher-order advantages.

Lower-order advantages come from the use of inexpensive resources: staff, raw materials (375 gold, synthetic stones, pieces without stones, chains), advertising. They are lower-order because they are very unstable and can easily be lost, either through rising prices and wages or because competitors can use the same cheap resources. In other words, lower-order advantages cannot ensure lasting superiority over competitors.

Higher-order advantages include the originality of the piece, the uniqueness of the gemstones used, highly qualified specialists and a strong company reputation.

The last is especially valuable — a good reputation. It is achieved with great difficulty and requires large spending to maintain.

Use different strategies depending on your market position

Option A. You are a small company. What weapons can you use against large competitors?

  1. Be independent. Use the freedom to take important decisions without submitting them for approval. Independence and broad authority carry enormous psychological weight.
  2. Be flexible. Break the conventional limits of the working day. When people set their own hours, they work better and longer and feel happier through the day.
  3. Be tolerant of employees’ eccentricities.
  4. Speed up decision-making. In large companies with traditions of general meetings and collective leadership, decisions can take weeks or months. The worst decision is no decision.
  5. Think about the atmosphere you create for people. Make sure your best employees are happy. Managers must constantly think about how to improve the working atmosphere and inspire staff — the “servant leadership” style now very fashionable in the West.
  6. Practise universal involvement in the work. Many people, especially young ones, want more authority among colleagues and fuller involvement in all the firm’s affairs. They are not satisfied when products or services are promoted without them.

Option B. Your competitor is a small firm, and you are a large, stable company with a well-known brand. What weapons can you use against newcomers and small companies?

Leading large companies have already learned to imitate small ones. They create small autonomous units in which the best employees, regardless of age and rank, have more opportunities to build capital. Such companies make sure employees keep in touch with the main organisation and have access to management — all of which makes them feel part of the company.

A large company can benefit from its size. It has more money, so it can offer talented employees higher, better-paid positions. Large companies have more vacancies; more vacancies mean more managers. So large companies offer more opportunities for training.

Assortment and packaging

Having built the assortment, focus on product groups at different stages of the life cycle. The following strategic decisions are recommended for each group of designs:

  1. Exclusive pieces of original design. This group must be protected and strengthened, using part of the income from serial production.
  2. Slow-selling serial products. Where possible, get rid of this group or look for ways to sustain demand (lower costs, more advertising).
  3. Serial products with stable demand (chains, traditional pieces without stones). Strict control of investment in their production is needed, and surplus cash should go to developing a new collection.
  4. Original pieces using new finishing technologies. The company should explore turning them into its exclusive collection.

When analysing the competitiveness of your jewellery and planning the assortment, answer these questions:

  1. How competitive is each design in the market as a whole and in particular segments?
  2. How and why, given the competition, should the assortment be expanded or reduced?
  3. Into which market segment should new designs be promoted, and why?
  4. Which designs should be discontinued, and why?

Summary and exercises

Key points

  • The value of a piece is made up of metal and stones, design, quality of workmanship, brand and service.
  • An assortment is built from the customer and the company’s market position, not from what is convenient to produce.
  • A competitive advantage is chosen deliberately — price, design, quality, speed or service — and then held.
  • Packaging is part of the product and the brand: it sells, protects and is remembered.

Review questions

  1. Which factors make up the value of a piece of jewellery for the buyer?
  2. Which approaches to building an assortment do you know?
  3. How does the choice of strategy depend on the company’s position — leader, challenger, niche player?
  4. What functions does branded packaging perform?

Practical task

Analyse the assortment of one jewellery store: divide it into 4–5 groups, find the gaps and suggest what to add and what to remove.

Test yourself

The customer pays for the whole package.

2. What should an assortment be built from?

An assortment is built from demand and strategy.
